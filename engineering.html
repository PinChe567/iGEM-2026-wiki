Abstract

Engineering changed the mammalian expression backbone, reporter architecture, fluorescence reader and decoder evaluation. Sequence review led to pcDNA3.1(+); a weak IRES reporter prompted a GCaMP6f–Orco fusion. Circuit review removed a redundant processor and improved weak-signal protection, while optical and gas models guided the enclosure. Decoder audits exposed preprocessing and clock errors. Six receptor-design iterations tested prediction, mutation ranking and molecular access in a separate mosquito reference complex.

How to read the cycles

Each cycle connects a design question, a built artifact, evidence and a revision. Evidence includes sequence reviews, qualitative observations, computations and measurements.

The experimental host is HEK293. Assay procedures are documented in Experiments and image-level evidence in Results. Detailed physical models, decoder methods and receptor-engineering analyses are kept on Model; build files and component choices are on Hardware.

Cycle 2 Orco–IRES–GCaMP6 gave a very weak signal

Evidence status DATA

Which part of the Orco–calcium–reporter chain could explain the weak green response?

Qualitative observation; mechanism remains a hypothesis

Evidence → Learning → Change

Evidence
VUAA1 produced a faint green-fluorescence increase, too weak to quantify. The observation prompted review of reporter abundance and localization.
Learning
Initial hypothesis (poor Orco expression) was challenged by construct-map re-examination: GCaMP6 is IRES-downstream, so reduced reporter abundance is a plausible explanation for the weak signal — a mechanistic hypothesis, not a measured diagnosis.
Change
Redesign the reporting module as a translational fusion (Cycle 3) to restore 1:1 stoichiometry and pore-proximal sensing.

Design

The bicistronic cassette placed Orco upstream of an IRES and GCaMP downstream: CMV → Orco → STOP → IRES → GCaMP → STOP. Separate proteins preserved the bicistronic arrangement used in the visibly fluorescent OR–IRES–mCherry modules.

We tested whether downstream GCaMP provided enough signal for a transient calcium response.

Build

We synthesized Orco–IRES–GCaMP and transfected HEK293. Orco and cytosolic GCaMP remained separate proteins, with GCaMP translated downstream of the IRES.

Test

Test evidence: Preliminary reporter observation; quantitative comparison not archived.

We challenged the pathway with VUAA1, an Orco agonist [2]. The green-fluorescence increase was visible on close inspection but too weak for reliable quantification.

This observation did not resolve receptor abundance, localization, reporter abundance, calcium handling or imaging limitations.

Learn

The map placed Orco in the cap-dependent upstream position and GCaMP downstream of the IRES, suggesting a reporter-expression bottleneck [3]. GCaMP fluorescence also depends on calcium state [4]; a dim resting image cannot by itself establish failed expression.

Reporter proximity to the channel was a second hypothesis. Neither abundance nor localization was measured independently.

Redesign

We chose a single-ORF GCaMP6f–linker–Orco fusion to address translation stoichiometry and channel proximity together. A 2A design would still produce separate proteins.

Because several features changed together, any fusion comparison concerns the whole redesign.

Key decision

Selected a single-ORF, pore-proximal reporter architecture.

Design

We placed GCaMP6f at the Orco N terminus through a flexible (GGGGS)₃ linker to coordinate translation and place the reporter near the channel. Cytosolic N-terminal placement follows insect OR/Orco topology [5] [6].

Synonymous codons diversified the three repeated 15-bp linker units while retaining the peptide sequence.

The final 8,293-bp pcDNA3.1(+) plasmid was synthesised, the vendor sequence matched the intended construct, and the part was recorded as BBa_26E11Z80.

These fixed images document fluorescence detectability, not a live calcium-response comparison.

Sequence correctness, detectable fluorescence and dynamic sensor function are separate outcomes. Dim resting GCaMP6f and crowded nuclei limit simple cell-fraction estimates.

Cycle 1 From a Complete Circuit to a Measurable Circuit

▣ Evidence status REVIEW-SUPPORTED

Would the first circuit preserve a weak photodiode signal through to digitization?

Electrical design audit and professional review

Evidence → Learning → Change

Evidence
August 2 V1 PCB archive Team archive: 73 DRC violations; 4 unconnected items; 7 ERC errors; 11 ERC warnings (Figure E-H1B). Followed by iST Group schematic/layout review. Manufacturing of V1 was stopped.
Learning
High gain is not the same as high sensitivity. At 100 MΩ, contamination, humidity, flux residue, incorrect guarding, or nearby switching can rival the photodiode current. A high-resolution ADC cannot recover information corrupted before digitization.
Change
Objective shifted from “amplify fluorescence as much as possible” to preserve measurement integrity across the analog/optical chain — driving the concrete V2 redesign listed under Redesign.

Conceptual pivot

Before

“Amplify the fluorescence as much as possible”

↓

After

“Preserve measurement integrity throughout the entire analog and optical chain.”

Hardware V1 → V2 (measurement integrity)

Showing Before · V1

Gain-first V1

  • 100 MΩ TIA emphasis for maximum amplification
  • Longer high-impedance PD/TIA routing risk
  • Shared analog/digital supply priorities
  • Stopped before fab after DRC/ERC + review

Integrity-first V2

  • Shorter PD–LMP7721 path + guarded region
  • ADS8866 symbol rebuilt; 3-wire CS + damping
  • Separate 3V3_ANA / 3V3_DIG rails
  • Improved decoupling and return-current management
Design

V1 combined LED excitation, a sample, photodiode, LMP7721 TIA, ADS8866 ADC and ESP32. The four-layer board included 100 MΩ feedback, four LED channels, power regulation and a Raspberry Pi interface.

The review examined whether layout, supplies and the ADC interface preserved the signal at this gain.

Build

By early August, V1 had a schematic, PCB layout, BOM, footprints, 3D models and DRC/ERC reports. The board was not fabricated.

The team prepared the design; iST Group reviewed the high-impedance detector region and mixed-signal interfaces.

Test

Test evidence: Archived computational DRC / ERC audit.

The 2 August archive recorded 73 DRC violations, four unconnected items, seven ERC errors and 11 ERC warnings, covering clearance, solder mask, incomplete nets, guards, power rules and symbol/footprint consistency. V1 was not fabrication-ready.

Professional review covered LMP7721 pins and guarding, photodiode pads, TIA placement, ADS8866/reference decoupling, SAR input behavior, ESP32 EN/BOOT and critical capacitors.

Learn

At 100 MΩ, leakage from long traces, poor guarding, humidity or flux residue can rival photocurrent. More ADC resolution cannot recover a corrupted analog signal.

The ADC symbol also required correction against its datasheet. The design objective shifted from gain alone to control of optical and electrical interference.

Redesign

V2 shortens the photodiode–LMP7721 high-impedance path and adds guarding around the detector/TIA feedback region. The ADC symbol was rebuilt from the ADS8866 pinout; the design uses the intended three-wire CS arrangement with DIN high and damping on SCLK/CONVST. Analog and digital 3.3 V supplies, decoupling and return-current paths were separated or reorganized.

Cycle 2 then reviewed the need for a second processor.

Key decision

Stop V1 before fab; treat DRC/ERC plus professional review as the cycle’s Test evidence; redesign V2 around measurement integrity rather than maximum gain.

▣ Evidence status REVIEW-SUPPORTED

Which tasks truly require an additional computer inside the reader?

Architecture review; portable operation unvalidated

Evidence
Function-by-function mapping showed the ESP32 already provides GPIO, SPI, UART, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and enough embedded computation for acquisition and digital lock-in Espressif · ESP32 datasheet. Heavier NeuroAI analysis can run on an external server.
Learning

Change

Architecture before / after

Showing Before · V1

ESP32 + Raspberry Pi

  • ESP32: LED timing, ADC acquisition, low-level processing
  • Raspberry Pi: higher-level control, networking, computation
  • Power concept depended on the Raspberry Pi 5 V supply
  • Reader framed as a PCB attached to another computer

  • Raspberry Pi removed — no unique measurement role remained
  • ESP32 retains acquisition, DLIA-class processing, and wireless links

Design

V1 assigned acquisition and signal processing to ESP32, with Raspberry Pi for control, networking and heavier computation.

We mapped excitation, ADC acquisition, demodulation, user interaction and data export to the available processors.

Build

The V1 package documented both processors and their space, power and interface requirements.

ESP32 GPIO, serial and wireless interfaces covered reader control, acquisition and a local UI. Heavy NeuroAI analysis remained external.

Test

Test evidence: Architecture design review.

The function review found no unique measurement task for Raspberry Pi. ESP32 specifications supported acquisition, control and the proposed digital lock-in design.

This was an architecture review, not an assembled-system benchmark.

Learn

Removing the second processor reduced board area, connectors, supply demands, enclosure height and software boundaries.

Redesign

V2 retained ESP32 and removed Raspberry Pi. Its power design uses USB-C, single-cell charging/power-path management, a battery and a 5 V boost path.

The simplified board set the enclosure constraints. Component and power details: Hardware.

Key decision

Drop the Raspberry Pi; keep measurement and lock-in on the ESP32; move heavy NeuroAI off-board; redesign power for portable standalone operation.

Cycle 4 Use optical and gas models to constrain the enclosure

How should a compact shared cartridge guide light and sample gas before it is fabricated?

Optical and gas computations completed

Design

The enclosure brief required a compact footprint, real connector access, a fan below the PCB, black-sleeved flat wells, fewer printed parts and a clear assembly sequence. The resulting nominal envelope is 108 × 108 × 66 mm, with LED-based well positions and a removable seal-loading plate.

We modeled collection geometry, cross-well transfer and gas redirection within the shared enclosure.

Build

The package contains editable CAD and individual STLs for eight operating prints, including an integrated fan cradle and gas deflector. The optical model represents clear flat-bottom wells, the off-center detector proxy, filter/seal and opaque carrier. The gas model represents the common plenum and alternative perforated or directional features.

Parameter sweeps, raw outputs and illustrated manuals accompany the build sources. Published simulations retain their 100 µL liquid assumption; the later operating document proposes 80 µL. These are different liquid-volume configurations.

Test

Test evidence: Conditional computational comparison.

Under its stated assumptions, the ideal NE optical case predicts 0.593 ± 0.035 nW. Intended-well fractions of 88.6–90.9% indicate cross-well optical transfer even with frequency-separated excitation.

For the selected gas geometry, shelf_w8_e0p2 reduces the downward momentum proxy by 36.1% on the finest 0.20 mm grid. The fixed-STL Q3 candidate gives a worst gas t90 of 50.00 s, compared with 50.98 s in the selected-grid check. These predictions describe gas equilibration, not cell-plane exposure.

Subsequent spectral comparison. The synthetic detector-current model predicts that one LEE 101 layer retains 70.43% of green-proxy current and 2.987% of LED current; two layers retain 51.27% and 0.410%. The filter comparison documents the spectrum and current model.

Learn

Flat well floors still produced nonuniform excitation, and the detector active area was off-center. Cross-well transfer limits direct assignment of recovered signals to samples.

The gas comparison favored controlled redirection at low Reynolds number. Membrane and liquid transport add delays beyond the modeled plenum response.

Redesign

The models informed a fixed optical layout, an opaque carrier/filter arrangement and the selected shelf geometry. The 3 mL/min candidate applies to the modeled geometry and flow assumptions.

Cycle 5 documents the manuals and evaluation specification. Hardware presents the result figures and their design consequences.

The selected design uses one LEE 101 layer. The Edmund #29-190 comparison uses a constructed OD4 curve. See the updated filter design decision.

Decision carried forward

Selected the computed optical arrangement, shelf geometry and one-layer LEE 101 filter.

Design

Build

Dry Lab · Computational receptor engineering

Six receptor design iterations

Six computational iterations connect response prediction, mutation audits and membrane simulations. Or22a and Or35a remain functional candidates; no engineered receptor demonstrated improved sensitivity or selectivity.

Complete receptor models, 36 figure groups and downloadable data →

Cycle 1 Turn odor-response data into design candidates

Design

Curate a benchmark of 24 Drosophila receptors and 110 odorants from published measurements. Compare a project-defined panel of mold-related volatiles with control odorants; these compounds are not assumed to be unique markers of mold. (Hallem & Carlson, 2006)

Build

Combine molecular descriptors, receptor sequences and AlphaFold-derived structural features in matched prediction models. Generate Or22a (Hallem & Carlson, 2006) / Or35a point-mutation models with ColabFold and dock target and control odorants. The build consists of computational sequences and structures. (Jumper et al., 2021; Mirdita et al., 2022)

Test

For receptors excluded from training, R² increased from 0.1125 with ligand features to 0.2325 with sequence features. The full representation reached 0.2356; its additional gain had an uncertainty interval spanning zero. An initial docking (Trott & Olson, 2010) screen suggested small relative preference shifts for F34Y and E196Q.

Learn

Prediction prioritized candidates; docking differences did not establish selectivity. R² measures predictive fit.

Redesign

Move from ranking candidates to checking whether the proposed mutations actually contact the odorant and whether their ranking survives changes in docking setup.

Inspect this cycle’s figures, methods and limits →

Cycle 2 Test whether a better docking score supports selectivity

Design

Require both an interpretable ligand–residue relationship and a preference that is stable across reasonable docking (Trott & Olson, 2010) choices.

Build

Align mutant and wild-type structures, compare a common search region with an alternative template-informed center, and inspect side-chain contacts. Define preference as the docking change for controls minus the change for mold-related odorants.

Test

Using a 4.5 Å contact criterion, neither F34Y nor E196Q supported a direct-contact explanation in the inspected poses. Candidate preference directions were sensitive to docking setup and odor-panel composition. No mutation maintained a robust positive preference across these checks.

Learn

Neither direct contact nor robust docking preference supported an improved receptor.

Redesign

Separate the functional-prediction question from the physical-access question. Use the OR10 (Zhao et al., 2024)/Orco complex to test what docking alone leaves unresolved.

Inspect this cycle’s figures, methods and limits →

Cycle 3 Ask whether receptor motion improves odor ranking

Design

Use experimental unbound 8V00 and o-cresol-bound 8V02 structures as OR10/Orco references. Reconstructed unresolved loops are model assumptions, not experimentally observed coordinates. (Zhao et al., 2024)

Build

Prepare the receptor complex in a hydrated POPC membrane with ions. Correct geometric and periodic-box problems before extending bound-state diagnostics to 20 ns and sampling unbound pockets with 5 ns MD (Eastman et al., 2024). Cluster the pockets and dock odorants into representative conformations.

Test

In an expanded panel of 29 dockable odorants, Spearman correlations were 0.407 and 0.338 for two MD-derived model ensembles, compared with 0.450 for static 8V00 and 0.504 for static 8V02. Responses were digitized from a published calcium assay. An exploratory aromatic-ring-count baseline reached 0.790, highlighting chemical-panel effects. (Bohbot et al., 2011)

Learn

Retrospective correlations are not sensitivity, EC50 or affinity measurements. Assays were analyzed separately; short MD does not establish equilibrium.

Redesign

Test molecular access explicitly, beginning with unbiased trajectories of o-cresol placed outside the apo receptor.

Inspect this cycle’s figures, methods and limits →

Cycle 4 Reduce the distance between an odorant and its candidate entrance

Design

Distinguish reaching the receptor surface from actually occupying its binding pocket.

Build

Place o-cresol (Zhao et al., 2024) in external water, at the water–membrane interface, and near the candidate S3/S6 entrance. Compare low- and high-occupancy solvent preparations to explore encounter frequency, then reduce the initial diffusion distance directly.

Test

Pocket-distance, contact and occupancy tracking showed surface/membrane encounters but no sustained entry.

Learn

A nearby starting position does not guarantee a passable entrance. A missing event over nanoseconds cannot prove that natural entry is impossible.

Redesign

Use the experimentally observed bound pose to explore outward motion and test receptor-side opening motions separately.

Inspect this cycle’s figures, methods and limits →

Cycle 5 Test whether opening the entrance and waiting permits release

Design

Separate direct ligand pulling from receptor-side bias, and compare guided entrance motion with an unsteered control under the same observation schedule.

Build

Use steered molecular dynamics (SMD (Isralewitz et al., 2001)) for exploratory outward motion. In a separate test, bias S3/S6 (Zhao et al., 2024) reference distances toward apo values while leaving o-cresol free of direct pulling. Extend the receptor-guidance protocol to a total of 70 ns, with the final 1 ns after bias removal.

Test

Assess entrance distances and ligand departure separately. Neither the guided nor the unsteered 70 ns trajectories met the sustained-exit criterion. The initial outward SMD attempts did not yield an accepted exit route for reverse-entry testing.

Learn

Neither longer observation nor receptor guidance produced a sustained exit. These biased 70 ns records do not measure natural residence time.

Redesign

Replace a single opening-distance proxy with explicit position-and-orientation searches for the whole odorant, including nearby lipids.

Inspect this cycle’s figures, methods and limits →

Cycle 6 Move from empty space to a testable molecular pathway

Design

Distinguish a point-sized probe, a full rigid molecule and a flexible molecular environment. A geometrical centerline alone cannot establish passage.

Build

Identify a lateral S3/S6 (Zhao et al., 2024) corridor and a lower-membrane-coordinate corridor. Search the translation and rotation of all 16 o-cresol atoms with the receptor and lipids fixed. Test route-following SMD (Isralewitz et al., 2001) with mobile surroundings, extend local waiting from 0.5 to 2 ns, then repeat the rigid searches across 24 conformations from the 70 ns trajectories.

Test

No tested static conformation provided a complete collision-free route. Flexible pulling sometimes showed transient catch-up to the target, but no sustained release. In the least-repulsive selected protein-plus-lipid route, L128, Q292 and S291 and nearby lipids remained collision sources. The repulsion score diagnoses clashes; it is not a free-energy barrier.

Learn

Two finite corridors and related snapshots were tested; separate favorable segments do not form an observed trajectory.

Redesign

The resulting comparison design separates ligand displacement, local relaxation and a no-pull control; it has no test outcome.

Inspect this cycle’s figures, methods and limits →
