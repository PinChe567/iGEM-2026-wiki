Introduction

"Everyone has the right to education." Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 26 [1]

In 1948 the United Nations General Assembly adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Article 26 states that everyone has the right to education, that it shall be free at least in the elementary and fundamental stages, and that it "shall be directed to the full development of the human personality and to the strengthening of respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms" [1]. The 2030 Agenda carries the same commitment forward as Sustainable Development Goal 4, "ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all", and Target 4.7 asks that every learner gain the knowledge and skills needed to promote sustainable development [2].

Access to information is not the same as education that changes what people do. Science-communication research has long questioned the "deficit model", the assumption that people hold mistaken views because they lack facts. Knowledge does shape attitudes, but the relationship is far more nuanced than the model assumes [3], and the model persists for reasons that include how scientists are trained and how they imagine their audiences [4]. Research on pro-environmental behavior documents the same distance between knowing and doing [5].

Our own survey pointed the same way. Most respondents already understood that visible mold is a problem, so a lecture saying "mold is dangerous" would have repeated what many people knew. The gap was in the risks that cannot be seen: only 58% understood that a normal look and smell does not mean no risk, and even respondents with higher knowledge often did not act on it. We went looking for what the research suggests works instead: active learning raised exam performance by 0.47 standard deviations and lowered failure rates (21.8% against 33.8%) across 225 science, engineering, and mathematics studies [6], and in a meta-analysis of serious-games studies, serious games outperformed conventional instruction on learning (d = 0.29) and retention (d = 0.36), though they were not more motivating [7]. These studies come from formal courses, so we treat them as direction, not proof, and we test our own tools on our own audiences.

Synthetic biology adds a second reason to engage. The Royal Academy of Engineering's early public dialogue on the field called for an active and ongoing public engagement programme, with platforms for people to share their views on both the benefits and the concerns [8]. AeroSense puts a fly olfactory receptor into a cultured human cell line and reads it with a fly-inspired circuit. We wanted people to understand that, and to tell us what they thought of it.

Antennal Lobe: input (broadest reach)

Biological meaning. The antennal lobe is the fly's primary olfactory processing center — roughly 50 anatomically distinct glomeruli, each wired to a single receptor type, where signal from thousands of olfactory sensory neurons first converges. Nothing is interpreted yet here; it's simply where every receptor channel's raw reading arrives in parallel. It's the literal starting point of "a smell is a pattern across many channels" — the idea our whole sensing platform, and Module 1 (Discover) of our online Learning Lab, is built on.

How we reach this stage of education. We treat the AL as our broadest-reach layer. None of the three projects here are trying to teach anyone anything in depth — they're trying to find out what the widest possible audience already believes (Public Survey), get them to make a decision and see its consequence in under fifteen minutes (Online Games), or keep the same corrective claim in front of them through feeds, short videos, and articles they already read (Social Media, Short Video & Articles). Like the AL itself, breadth comes before interpretation.

1. Public Survey

Why does it matter?

Before designing any education, we needed to know what people actually believed about mold and mycotoxin risk, and whether knowing the facts changes what they do. We did not want to assume a knowledge gap and then build a solution for a problem that might not exist in the form we imagined.

Educational Impact from us.

We ran a bilingual (Chinese and English) online questionnaire on mold and mycotoxin understanding, food-storage habits and what people want from a screening tool. It reached 300 respondents. Three findings shaped the rest of our education work:

Public understanding varies by risk concept. People were most secure about the risk they can see. 78% understood that removing visible mold does not always make food safe. Understanding was lower for heat, where 64% understood that heating does not always destroy all mycotoxins, and lowest for looks and smell, where only 58% understood that a normal look and smell does not mean no risk. On that last item 27% were unsure and 15% held the misconception. The risks that cannot be seen are the ones people understand least, and they are the risks our sensor is meant to address.

Knowledge does not automatically translate into practice. We compared respondents with higher knowledge (n = 180) with those with lower or uncertain knowledge (n = 120). Higher-knowledge respondents were more likely to record the opening date (42% against 25%) and to report a recommended storage practice (71% against 46%). But even in the higher-knowledge group, 58% did not record the opening date. Knowing the facts helped, but it did not close the gap between knowing and doing.

Users value trustworthy, actionable results. Asked what they need from a screening tool, respondents chose trustworthiness of results (76%) and accuracy, including avoiding false negatives (73%) first, followed by clear next-step guidance (68%). These ranked ahead of ease of use (61%), affordable cost per test (54%) and a fast result (49%). People do not only want a number; they want to trust it and know what to do next.

Impact on us.

The survey changed what we built. Because understanding was weakest for invisible risks, we made the claim that heat and looks and smell cannot tell you whether food is safe the center of the game and of Module 2 of the Learning Lab, which use the same statements as the survey so that later results can be compared with this baseline. Because knowledge did not automatically become practice, we added habits, such as recording the opening date, to the curriculum and our posts alongside the facts. Because people want trust and a clear next step, the Learning Lab teaches what a screening result does and does not certify, and what to do after each result, and our design prioritizes avoiding missed risk.

What it cannot tell us.

These are self-reported answers from people who chose to respond, so they show where to look, not how common each belief or habit is across Taiwan.

Try the decision questions

Diagnosis

What would you decide?

  1. Claim 1

    Cutting off the visible mold makes the rest safe to eat.

    Do you agree or disagree with this statement?

    Scientific interpretation

    Disagree. Removing a visible colony does not automatically make the rest of a food safe. In some firm foods, public guidance allows cutting away a margin; in soft, moist, or porous foods, mycelium and some mycotoxins can extend beyond what you see. “Always safe” is the over-claim.

  2. Claim 2

    Heating or roasting fully eliminates mycotoxins.

    Do you agree or disagree with this statement?

    Scientific interpretation

    Disagree. Heat may inactivate molds without eliminating heat-stable mycotoxins. Killing or reducing the organism is not the same as removing the metabolite. “Fully eliminates” is not a safe general rule for ordinary cooking.

  3. Claim 3

    If it looks and smells normal, it’s not risky.

    Do you agree or disagree with this statement?

    Scientific interpretation

    Disagree. Appearance and smell cannot establish mycotoxin status. Absence of an off-odor does not prove absence of toxin; presence of mold does not by itself quantify a toxin. Sensory inspection is not an assay.

2. Online Games — Odor Pixel Suite

Why does it matter?

The survey data above is exactly why a lecture wouldn't work here: the gaps it revealed — trusting a printed date instead of tracking when a pack was opened, and not being sure whether heat or a sniff test can be trusted — are decision habits, not missing facts, and habits don't move from being told something twice. We needed an experience where the learner makes the decision and sees what it costs, which is also why this design choice ties directly back to the Education loop and the project's own motivation rather than appearing out of nowhere.

Educational Impact from us.

Odor Pixel Suite is three short games, built through a five-stage design journey. We report the journey as a template with the evidence each stage should produce:

  1. Draft design
  2. Internal playtest & refinement
  3. Public playtest & feedback
  4. Camp/workshop deployment
  5. Data + optional external release

3. Social Media

Why does it matter?

The survey and the game both need someone to seek them out: fill in a questionnaire, or sit down and play. Social media reaches the person who will do neither. It puts the same ideas in a feed people are already scrolling, trading depth for reach.

Educational Impact from us.

We ran three series, each built for a different kind of reader.

Series 1: From fly nose to edge device.

Three posts that follow one smell through the AeroSense system, one post per layer: the living sensor, the decoding model, and the hardware. Each post is a short carousel written for readers with no biology or engineering background.

Post 1, Parts Design: "We put a fruit fly's nose inside a human cell." Explains how a fruit fly odor receptor and a calcium-sensitive glow protein, placed in human cells, turn a smell into light: an odor molecule binds the receptor, calcium flows in, and the cell lights up. The brighter the glow, the stronger the smell.

Post 2, Model: "The cell lit up. Now what?" How a circuit modeled on the fruit fly brain turns the glow into a decision. Coming soon.

Post 3, Hardware: "Hearing a cell's faint glow in a noisy room." How the edge device picks a faint signal out of background noise.

Series 2: Sustainability Pop Quiz.

Four multiple-choice Instagram stories, each followed by an answer card. Every question ties one AeroSense design choice to a Sustainable Development Goal, so followers learn what the goals mean by guessing which one applies.

Flagging a batch of bananas or mangoes before disease symptoms are visible points to SDG 2, Zero Hunger. An edge classifier under 1 W, with core logic from a paper our own team published, points to SDG 9, Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure. A reusable, non-destructive sensing cartridge points to SDG 12, Responsible Consumption and Production. And the interviews with an e-nose company's chief scientist, several biotech companies, and an analytical-instrumentation professor point to SDG 17, Partnerships for the Goals.

Impact on us. We treat social media as a reach channel, not a measurement tool. Over the posting period, our feed posts reached more than 1,700 accounts, our stories more than 1,400, and our reels more than 500, with an average of about 50 likes per post. We gained more than 100 new followers, and more than 50 people answered the Sustainability Pop Quiz stories, getting about 75% correct on average. Our best-performing post was Post 2, "The cell lit up. Now what?" Most of our audience was aged 20 to 30.

These numbers show interest, not learning. Saves, poll responses, and comment questions cannot tell us whether someone understood an idea, so we measure knowledge change where we can pair a before and an after: in the game and the Learning Lab. Our own observation from comparable in-person outreach, such as street booths and pub-quiz events, is that this kind of content tends to over-index on audiences who are already interested in science. The age data does not settle this, because it tells us who followed, not whether they were already interested in science.

Mushroom Body Calyx: integration (structured learning)

Biological meaning.

The calyx is the dendritic input zone of the mushroom body — around 2,000 Kenyon cells receive sparse, combinatorial input here from antennal-lobe projection neurons, and this is where parallel receptor channels actually get bound together into one learnable, associative pattern. It's the integration step — the subject of Module 4 (Decode) of our curriculum — and biologically, it's where exposure becomes memory.

How we reach this stage of education.

The three projects here have a different job than the first part's: they're not trying to reach as many people as possible, they're trying to turn a shorter exposure into real, structured understanding, across the full age range our work touches — elementary schoolers, university life-science and medical students, and a self-paced option open to anyone.

1. Elementary School Science Camp

Why does it matter?

Shan-chi Elementary School is a rural school in Xinfeng Township, Hsinchu County. Bringing a hands-on science session to a school there is a small step toward the equal access to quality education that SDG 4 describes. The camp also lets us test our claim that the game can be understood by children, instead of assuming it.

Educational Impact from us.

On October 12, 2026 we ran a half-day science camp at the school for more than thirty upper-grade pupils (grades 5 and 6).

We started with a Science Mini-Class. In about ten minutes we asked three questions in a row: How does a nose smell? How does an electronic nose smell? And what does our lab do? Each answer built on the one before, so the children could follow a smell from their own nose, to a machine, to the sensor we are building.

Next, the children played Odor Pixel Suite, the game we designed, as a challenge that lasted about ten minutes. We adapted it for a whole class. Instead of one child at one screen, the questions were projected on a screen, and each team answered at the same time by holding up one, two, or three fingers.

Then came a drawing and worksheet session of about fifteen minutes. The children drew a cartoon nose or a cartoon fruit fly, and completed a worksheet. The children's drawings are shown in the Art Exhibition section below.

Impact on us.

We collected 22 worksheets from the camp, each asking what the child learned or felt. Thirteen children named something they had learned, and nine wrote only that they had fun or liked the game. Five wrote about the electronic nose, and five mentioned the game. One wrote that the memory game was "really hard," and another noted that it had "a lot of questions."

Several children put the science in their own words. One wrote that everyday smells are made of many tiny molecules. Another wrote that different animals smell in different ways and that smelling uses the brain, not only the nose. A third wrote that we can use the brain in place of the nose. One wrote that technology is powerful and should be used to build the future, and one asked the school to hold the activity again.

This suggests that children could follow the path from nose to electronic nose and enjoyed the game, although the worksheets are short and self-reported, so they do not measure how much each child understood.

2. Olfactory Neuroscience Workshop (with Prof. Chung-Chuan Lo)

Why does it matter?

Few people outside the field know how an animal actually turns a smell into a decision, and that logic is exactly what our electronic nose borrows. The workshop put the original science and our engineering in the same room, so students could see the line from how a brain smells to how we design a sensor. It also gave us a first chance to check whether the full online chain, talk, project and game, works with students who are one step from choosing a field.

Educational Impact from us.

On October 14, 2026, we ran "From the Brain to the Electronic Nose," a workshop for students of the NTHU College of Life Science and Medicine. Prof. Lo then spoke on the biological logic underneath olfaction: how receptors bind odor molecules, how a single smell is represented by a combination of receptors rather than one, and how the fly's antennal lobe and mushroom body turn that pattern into a decision. He also showed how this circuit-level understanding from computational neuroscience has inspired approaches to artificial intelligence.

We followed with introducing our project: why early detection of food mold and crop disease matters, how a living-cell sensor built from an olfactory receptor and a fluorescent reporter detects volatile compounds, and how a fly-brain-inspired classifier reads the resulting "odor fingerprint." We closed that part with the idea that how biology smells is how we design. Then everyone tried our online platform. We gave a short tour of the Learning Lab, running one module live so that students could see how its five modules fit together. The workshop ended with a feedback form.

Impact on us.

The workshop drew 15 participants, split evenly across doctoral, master's, and bachelor's students. Prof. Lo's talk received the highest satisfaction, and participants strongly agreed that they understood the olfactory mechanism and the electronic nose mechanism better after attending.

Their suggestions were specific. Some English technical terms were hard to follow, which made it difficult to understand the rest of the talk, remember where a term had appeared, and ask questions afterward. Participants wanted a take-home message that gives the overall picture of the project, and asked us to explain the neural network, the project goal, and the intended use more clearly.

3. AeroSense Learning Lab — the online curriculum

Why does it matter?

A camp or a workshop reaches the people in the room. We believe everyone should get the same chance to understand the science that affects their food, and that should not depend on living near a university. The Learning Lab lets anyone go through the whole chain at their own pace, from home: how a fly smells, how a living cell can be turned into a sensor, and what a screening result does and does not tell you.

Educational Impact from us.

  • Free to use online, with nothing to install
  • Five modules of about 8 minutes each, around 40 minutes in total, organized as Biology, then Engineering, then Decision
  • Module 2 uses the same statements as our public survey, so learners' answers can be compared with our survey baseline
  • Printable discussion prompts for educators, and a teacher toolkit that other teams can build on

Live at Learning Lab · AeroSense · NTHU iGEM 2026

We wrote it for self-learners from upper-secondary school onward, for university students, and for educators who want a ready-made lesson on olfaction and food safety. The modules can be taken in any order, but they are designed to follow the logic of the project itself, as listed below.

  1. Discover. How animals smell: receptors binding odor molecules, and sparse, combinatorial coding in the fly antennal lobe and mushroom body.
  2. Question. Mold versus mycotoxin risk, and why looks and smell cannot be trusted.
  3. Build. Expressing OR/Orco and GCaMP6 in cultured cells to make a self-renewing living sensor.
  4. Decode. A smell as a pattern across many receptors, and how fly-brain-inspired computation reads it.
  5. Decide. Screening is not certified testing: risk tiers and the correct next action.

Each module ends with three check questions. The full set is in the teacher toolkit, described under Teacher Toolkit & Education Guide in Mushroom Body Lobes / MBON.

01 · DiscoverHow do we smell?

From volatile molecules to receptor patterns.

 02 · QuestionCan a smell answer a safety question?

Practise the limits of heat, appearance and odor as evidence.

 03 · BuildHow could a cell become a sensor?

Connect receptor, reporter and measurement.

 04 · DecodeWhat does the pattern mean?

Use the fly circuit as an entry point into computational interpretation.

 05 · DecideWhat should happen next?

Use uncertainty, screening and confirmation to choose a responsible action.

Impact on us.

More than 500 people have gone through the Learning Lab. Because Module 2 reuses the survey's statements word for word, their answers can be compared with our survey baseline of 300 respondents.

Learners told us two things, and we changed the platform in response. First, the interface was unclear and the opening had too much text, so we cut the text and separated sections with color blocks to make the page easier to follow. Second, the opening was too difficult. To make the platform accessible to more people, we lowered the difficulty of the opening and closing sections and added hands-on tasks, so that learners understand by doing.

Such an online platform allows children to access science knowledge in a correct and fun way. by a Director of Student Affairs in Elementary

The modules stand alone, so another team or teacher can take one module, adapt it to their own project, or add a module of their own.

Mushroom Body Lobes / MBON: output (validation and handover)

Biological meaning.

Kenyon cell axons run through the mushroom body lobes, where mushroom body output neurons (MBONs) read the learned pattern and convert it into an actual decision — approach or avoid. It's the output stage — the subject of Module 5 (Decide) of our online Learning Lab — and biologically, it's where internal learning becomes visible, external behavior.

How we reach this stage of education.

The projects here aren't about reach or depth — they're about what happens when our work gets read out to an outside expert audience and has to hold up: an industry conference, a peer iGEM team, and an academic lecture hall. It also holds the two ways our work leaves the room: a toolkit other teachers can run, and an exhibition of what participants made.

1. SynBioBeta 2026 — Next-Gen BioLeaders & Poster Session

Why does it matter?

Everything else in this section teaches people who came to us. SynBioBeta reverses the direction. It is where working synthetic biologists, founders and investors evaluate each other's work, and the Next-Gen BioLeaders program puts emerging leaders in the room with that community. For an education project, that was a chance to be the learner, and to test whether our story, "a smell is a pattern, read by a fly-inspired circuit," can be understood by non-specialists in a few minutes at a poster.

Educational Impact from us.

At SynBioBeta 2026 (San Jose, May 4 to 7), as one of the Next-Gen BioLeaders, with our poster accepted and presented in the Expo Hall. The poster, "Development of a Bionic Olfactory Edge Computing System Integrating Drosophila Neural Models and Live-Cell Sensor Arrays," fit the whole platform on one page for a mixed audience: why conventional electronic noses fall short, how the fly solves the problem, and why we use living cells.

The poster showed us where the explanation broke. A PhD student from UCLA, with a wet-lab background, told us he had not known how a live-cell sensor is actually built. That was the step we had explained least.

Impact on us.

The hardest question we were asked was whether our living-cell system can scale. Two more followed: how a medically positioned device would be regulated, and whether the hardware has enough margin. They came from people who evaluate companies, not student projects.

Our formal Human Practices work had not yet begun in May, so this was our first read on the project from outside the lab, and the questions landed on the wet lab, the model and the hardware. They changed our plan and led us to add consultations.

2. Mini Jamboree (with International iGEM community)

Why does it matter?

Most of our audiences so far were people who know little about our field. Other iGEM teams are the opposite: they know what a weak result looks like, and they run education projects of their own. A cross-border meeting with them gave us two things we could not get from our own audiences, expert criticism of our design and a look at how other teams teach.

Educational Impact from us.

On October 8, 2026, iGEM TAU-Israel hosted an online Mini Jamboree. Eight teams from six countries took part: NCKU-Tainan and NTHU from Taiwan, CAU-China and AIS-China from China, CERNAL from Israel, Cell Forge from India, iGEM-UA Alicante from Spain, and UFRGS-Brazil. Each team presented for about fifteen minutes, so every team heard seven other projects and every project was heard by teams in different regions with different regulations, farming and school systems. Besides our project, we also shared the education work described on this page, so that our activities could be compared with theirs.

Impact on us.

We received feedback on our model design from teams outside our own circle, and we exchanged education activities with them.

3. China Medical University Guest Lecture

Why does it matter?

The circuit our classifier is built on comes from research co-published by our advisor, Prof. Chung-Chuan Lo, and Prof. Ching-Che Charng of China Medical University (Cheng et al., Science Advances, 2025). Prof. Charng invited us to bring that shared research back to his university, to an audience that could judge how we had used it.

Educational Impact from us.

On September 23, 2026, a member of our model team gave a research-sharing lecture on our electronic-nose work to more than thirty undergraduate and graduate students at China Medical University. The lecture followed one thread: why electronic noses drift and why recalibrating them is costly; how the insect olfactory system keeps its odor code stable; and how our spiking neural network borrows those ideas. We also presented the results that do not flatter the model: the 14 sensors in our test dataset behave like only three types, adding more features did not help once the data had been compressed, and accuracy fell to about 75% when odors were presented continuously. We taught the evidence together with its limits so that listeners could judge the method themselves.

Impact on us.

Most of the audience's questions were about how the model processes an odor: how sensor readings become a pattern, and how that pattern becomes a decision. They showed us that this step needs a plainer explanation than we had given. We changed how we introduced the model in the olfactory neuroscience workshop that followed.

4. Art Exhibition

We asked participants of the elementary school science camp, the olfactory neuroscience workshop and other activities to draw "the electronic nose and the fruit fly as you imagine them," and we treat their drawings as a creative output of our education work. What a learner draws shows what they took away in their own terms, which a quiz cannot show. The drawings are displayed on our Art Gallery page

5. Teacher Toolkit & Education Guide — open resources

Teacher Toolkit. When we met the principal of the elementary school where we ran our science camp, he told us he liked the way we teach and asked for a way to keep using it, since we cannot visit every year. We packaged the camp into a teacher toolkit: the lesson slides, the Odor Pixel Suite game, and instructions for using the Learning Lab, so that a teacher can run the same session without us. The school welcomed the approach, and the toolkit is free for any teacher to download.

Education Guide. The Education Guide is written for other teams, as a PDF on our Contribution page. It sets out the steps we followed: find out what your audience believes, choose one claim, match the format to the audience, measure the same way each time, and hand the work on, so that a team that has never met us can plan its own education work.

Closing

We started this track by testing an assumption instead of trusting it — and found that the honest, narrow answer ("most people already know mold is dangerous; a few still trust heat or their nose to check it") was more useful than the broad one we'd assumed going in. Every project on this page, from a three-game suite to an art exhibition, exists to close the loop on that one specific, testable claim — carried from first contact (Region 1), through structured learning (Region 2), to outside validation and handover (Region 3).
