    Why identical wording — and why that is not enough

    Benefit. On Chain A, Module 2 reuses the survey’s three statements with the same item IDs. That is for cross-instrument comparability: a diagnostic baseline and a later pre/post can be read against each other instead of against three unrelated quizzes.

    Caution. Same-item pre/post testing can partly measure familiarity with the original sentence. If a future consented cohort is collected, we recommend one parallel “transfer” item per concept — a new wording of the same idea — to test whether learning generalizes. No transfer-item results exist, and none are shown.

    Odor Pixel Suite is not in that wording match. Its teaching target is olfactory coding. Comparability across Chain A instruments does not license a combined “education impact” score with Chain B.

    Assumption

    Why we changed the question

    Every education program starts with an assumption about what people do not know. Ours started with the assumption that is easy to make about food safety: that the public broadly misunderstands mold and mycotoxin risk, and that the job is to explain, more loudly, that mold is dangerous.

    We tested that assumption before building Track A. The three misconception items below are the diagnostic; try each decision first — sample counts appear after each choice. Item-level counts and caveats are authoritative here only.

    Diagnosis

    Before we show you the data — what would you decide?

    This is not a data-collection form and not part of our research sample. Choices stay in this browser tab. They are not stored, sent, logged, or added to n = 28.

    1. Claim 1

      Cutting off the visible mold makes the rest safe to eat.

      Do you agree or disagree with this statement?

      28 squares — one per respondent in the initial sample. They stay unmarked until you choose.

      Rejected the claim · Did not reject

      Scientific interpretation

      Disagree. Removing a visible colony does not automatically make the rest of a food safe. In some firm foods, public guidance allows cutting away a margin; in soft, moist, or porous foods, mycelium and some mycotoxins can extend beyond what you see. “Always safe” is the over-claim.

      Initial survey result

      27 of 28 rejected this statement (96%); 1 of 28 did not (4%).

      An initial diagnostic, not a population estimate (n = 28).

      Why this item mattered. Almost everyone in this sample already rejected the “cut it off” rule (27 of 28). A program that only repeated that warning would have taught a belief this group largely held.

    2. Claim 2

      Heating or roasting fully eliminates mycotoxins.

      Do you agree or disagree with this statement?

      28 squares — one per respondent in the initial sample. They stay unmarked until you choose.

      Rejected the claim · Did not reject

      Scientific interpretation

      Disagree. Heat may inactivate molds without eliminating heat-stable mycotoxins. Killing or reducing the organism is not the same as removing the metabolite. “Fully eliminates” is not a safe general rule for ordinary cooking.

      Initial survey result

      24 of 28 rejected this statement (86%); 4 of 28 did not (14%).

      An initial diagnostic, not a population estimate (n = 28).

      Why this item mattered. This is one of the two residual decision habits: 4 of 28 still treated heat as a complete safety check. Track A was built to correct that habit, not to announce that mold is dangerous.

    3. Claim 3

      If it looks and smells normal, it’s not risky.

      Do you agree or disagree with this statement?

      28 squares — one per respondent in the initial sample. They stay unmarked until you choose.

      Rejected the claim · Did not reject

      Scientific interpretation

      Disagree. Appearance and smell cannot establish mycotoxin status. Absence of an off-odor does not prove absence of toxin; presence of mold does not by itself quantify a toxin. Sensory inspection is not an assay.

      Initial survey result

      23 of 28 rejected this statement (82%); 5 of 28 did not (18%).

      An initial diagnostic, not a population estimate (n = 28).

      Why this item mattered. This is the second residual habit: 5 of 28 still treated look or smell as evidence of safety. That is the “it feels fine” moment a lecture does not interrupt.

    What the sample suggested

    The baseline came back higher than the assumption we started with. This is not a story of widespread ignorance. It is a narrower gap in two decision habits: treating heat as a complete safety check (4 of 28 did not reject that claim), and treating appearance or smell as a complete safety check (5 of 28 did not). A lecture that only repeats “mold is dangerous” would have targeted a belief most respondents in this sample already held.

    Limitation

    An initial diagnostic, not a population estimate.

    • n = 28. Counts on this page are counts over 28, not prevalence in Taiwan or in a general population.
    • Non-random sample. People were not drawn with a sampling frame that supports inference beyond this group.
    • General-consumer-skewed: 20 general consumers, 6 students, 2 other.
    • The 45–54 age bracket is overrepresented (10 of 28).
    • We used this to choose what to teach. We do not use it to estimate how common these beliefs are in the public.

    Interventions · Track A

    Better food-safety decisions

    Diagnose the residual claims, put a learner through a matched misconception challenge, then measure change when a real audience exists. This track is not the Odor Pixel Suite.

    Public survey — the diagnostic instrument

    • Delivery Completed
    • Evaluation Evidence available · directional only

    Four-module questionnaire (n = 28; 20 general consumers, 6 students, 2 other). Misconception-module item counts and sample caveats are presented once in Diagnosis — not repeated here.

    Storage behavior showed a related gap: only 8 of 28 respondents (29%) said they actively mark the date they open a package. Desiccant use was 3 of 28, compared with refrigeration (17 of 28) and sealed containers (16 of 28).

    On risk tolerance, 15 of 28 said they could not accept either kind of error, and 11 of 28 specifically could not accept a missed real risk (a false negative). 1 of 28 was more worried about a false alarm. These three figures sum to 27, not 28, so they are not presented here as a complete partition of the sample.

    On trust and pricing, respondents were far more worried about accuracy and credibility (18 and 17 mentions) than about price (10 mentions). The source draft also states that most respondents expected a screening tool to cost NT$150 or less per use, or to be free. That statement is given only as a percentage, not as a respondent fraction, so it is not converted into a count here.

    This survey did two jobs. It told Education what to teach. It also informed how AeroSense talks about risk output — a conservative low/medium/high tier rather than a binary safe/unsafe verdict — on the same evidence used here. That product-communication choice is recorded as a completed design decision, not as proof that learners later changed their minds.

    Interventions · Track B

    From curious visitor to synthetic-biology participant

    Understand a smell as a pattern, explore AeroSense’s biology-to-decision chain, then participate in a hands-on session. This track is not a second copy of the food-safety survey.

    Learn by playing · Track B

    Odor Pixel Suite

    Three games. Three ways to understand how smell becomes information.

    Instead of explaining olfactory coding only through text, we built three playable experiences that let learners recognize, navigate with, and decode odor patterns themselves. The suite teaches the sensing logic behind AeroSense: odor information is represented as patterns, patterns can guide decisions, mixtures create overlapping signals, and computational decoding is needed to interpret them. That move — from “a smell is just something we sense” to “odor is structured biological information that can be encoded and decoded” — is the Track B job. These games are educational illustrative models. They do not validate AeroSense sensor performance, and they are not a Track A heat/smell food-safety test.

    • Delivery Playable now
    • Evaluation Formal learning evaluation pending

    Game 01

    Pattern Recognition

    In-game title: Odor Pixel Lab

    Task. Read an LED scent-code pattern — sometimes with extra noise lights — and choose the matching odor.

    Learning idea. Odor identity is a multi-receptor pattern, not a single receptor’s reading.

    Text alternative: the player studies a grid of on/off cells that stands for a fictional receptor response, then picks which odor that pattern belongs to. Similar odors share more of the same cells. Extra lights are noise, not a new identity.

    Game 02

    AeroSense: Scentbound Labyrinth

    In-game title: AeroSense — Odor Maze

    Task (live hub copy). Walk a maze to the exit; dead-end chests unlock scent scan, guidance, and secret passages.

    Game 03

    Scent Mixer / Odor Fingerprint Mixer

    In-game title: Odor Fingerprint Mixer

    Task. Mix scents, read the overlapping receptor-response pattern, and work out which odors — and in what integer ratios — made the fingerprint.

    Learning idea. Mixed signals overlap; computational decoding compares candidates and ratios rather than reading a single peak.

    Text alternative: the player sees a 12-channel illustrative chart of a mixture, then infers which source odors are present and in what percentages. A text summary of the chart is provided so color is not the only cue. Solving the puzzle is not a real concentration measurement.

    Accessibility in the current suite

    The suite’s educator catalog documents keyboard play, visible focus, high-contrast and reduced-motion controls stored on-device, bilingual Traditional Chinese / English, and charts or grids that are not color-only. Documented keys are not the same in every Game 2 description: Game 1 uses 1–4; the live-hub maze copy documents WASD / Space / Q / E plus a touch joystick; the QC Shift educator file documents 1 / 2 / 3. This Education page is not an independent accessibility audit of the live builds. On this page, the three games remain understandable if the external site is down: titles, tasks, learning ideas, and text alternatives are all here.

    An early design hypothesis was that an interactive experience would also land on the residual heat and look/smell claims from the survey. Games 1 and 3, as shipped on the live hub, teach olfactory coding and mixture decoding. Game 2’s live hub copy is spatial scent navigation; the educator pack at the same URL documents a screening-shift role-play. Until a playtest instrument is shown to use the Track A item IDs, we do not treat the suite as a food-safety misconception intervention, and we do not treat Game 2’s two titles as one measured construct.

    Participation

    Where AeroSense’s next scientists begin

    Correcting a misconception is one educational task. Helping someone understand, question, and eventually participate in synthetic biology is another.

    AeroSense therefore combines self-paced learning, interactive games, and planned face-to-face activities into a deeper Track B pathway. Module 2 of the Learning Lab is the concise bridge back to Track A. Face-to-face sessions are planned; they have not run yet.

    Module 01 · Discover · Track B

    How do we smell?

    Lesson title in the Lab data: How do we smell?

    Core question
    How do humans and flies detect odors?
    What you learn
    Odor perception begins with volatile molecules at receptors; identity is a combinatorial pattern; sparse codes can help separate similar odors.
    Connection to AeroSense
    This is the biological inspiration: a compact receptor set representing odor as structured information, not one private switch.
    Assessment
    Three-item pre/post inside the module. Browser-local only. No published cohort.
    Duration
    ~8 min, as labeled on the live Learning Lab. Not a measured classroom time.

    ▣ Connected to our initial diagnostic

    If it looks fine, is it safe?

    Lesson title in the Lab data: Mold, Mycotoxins, and the Risk You Cannot See

    Core question
    When does mold become a food risk — and what can look, smell, or heat not settle?
    What you learn
    Distinguish mold growth from mycotoxin contamination. Test the three food-safety claims with evidence, not fear.
    Connection to AeroSense
    This is where Track A and Track B meet: the same diagnostic claims from the initial survey, inside the Lab’s pathway. The survey sample (n = 28) is directional, not a prevalence estimate. No paired learning gain is published.
    Assessment
    Three items worded to match the survey IDs MYTH_MOLD_REMOVE, MYTH_HEAT_DESTROY, and MYTH_LOOK_SMELL_SAFE. Same-item pre/post. No cohort table.
    Duration
    ~8 min, as labeled on the live Learning Lab.

    Open Module 2

    Module 03 · Build · Track B

    Can a cell learn to smell?

    Lesson title in the Lab data: Building a Living Odor Sensor

    Core question
    How can synthetic biology turn olfactory recognition into a living sensing system?
    What you learn
    OR, Orco, and GCaMP can be assembled into an educational sensing chain in cultured cells. Containment and biosafety are part of the design, not an afterthought.
    Connection to AeroSense
    Learners see the idea of a living sensor — receptor recognition converted into fluorescence — at an educational level. This is not a plasmid-construction tutorial and not a claim that a finished device is in hand.
    Assessment
    Three-item pre/post on living-cell sensing, component roles, and biosafety. No published cohort.
    Duration
    ~8 min, as labeled on the live Learning Lab.

    Open Module 3

    Module 04 · Decode · Track B

    An odor is a pattern

    Lesson title in the Lab data: Decoding an Odor Fingerprint

    Core question
    Why does recognizing an odor require more than one sensor value?
    What you learn
    Read a multi-receptor fingerprint. Contrast, sparse codes, and a classifier can change how separable a pattern is — they do not auto-validate a measurement.
    Connection to AeroSense
    This is the educational version of overlapping VOC responses and computational classification. Illustrative, not experimental sensor output.
    Assessment
    Three-item pre/post on pattern reading, representation, and uncertainty. No published cohort.
    Duration
    ~8 min, as labeled on the live Learning Lab.

    Want to experience pattern decoding instead of only reading about it? Play the Odor Pixel Suite.

    Open Module 4

    Module 05 · Decide · Track B

    A signal is not a verdict

    Lesson title in the Lab data: From Signal to Responsible Decision

    Core question
    What does a sensing result actually mean?
    What you learn
    Screening is not certified testing. Low / medium / high are action prompts. False positives, false negatives, and uncertainty change what a responsible next step can be.
    Connection to AeroSense
    Education should teach how not to misuse a signal: a screening band can guide follow-up; it cannot finish the chemical or legal question.
    Assessment
    Three-item pre/post on screening versus confirmation, risk-tier language, and next action. No published cohort.
    Duration
    ~8 min, as labeled on the live Learning Lab.

    Planned learning flow

    The exact timetable is not finalized. The intended flow follows the same science path as the live tools:

    1. Biological inspiration — how odor can be encoded (Module 1 / Game 1 ideas).
    2. Sensing principle — living-sensor concept at an educational level (Module 3).
    3. Interactive reasoning — play and discuss an Odor Pixel game.
    4. Interpretation of signals — patterns, overlap, and what a classifier cannot claim (Module 4).
    5. Limitations and application — screening is not a verdict (Module 5); optional Track A items if that session uses Module 2.

    This is an intended design, not a record of what we did.

    From learning science to doing science

    The goal is not only “we explained synthetic biology.” The intended progression is:

    1. Understand Explain how odor receptor systems represent a smell as a pattern. Live: Learning Lab Module 1 and Odor Pixel Lab.
    2. Question Challenge whether a sensing result, a look, or a smell actually proves safety. Live: Module 2 and the initial diagnostic. Planned: workshop discussion.
    3. Interpret Read a response pattern or a risk-tier output without treating it as a verdict. Live: Module 4, Module 5, Scent Mixer.
    4. Design Choose how an educational sensing challenge should be structured. Next-step goal. No current activity asks learners to design a new experiment or construct.
    5. Participate Discuss, test, or reason together in a facilitated room. Planned: university workshop and elementary camp. Current ceiling without those sessions: self-paced understand / question / interpret.

    Passive completion of a module is not the same as participation. We do not label the live Lab as “students are already doing science with us.”

    For educators: optional study mode

    Casual visitors play the suite immediately. There is no mandatory pre-test on the public Play path. That is the default learner experience.

    A separate workshop study mode can add paired measurement. Facilitators follow the education study protocol ↗(opens in a new tab): two short pre questions, the usual game, the same concepts after play plus one transfer item, optional feedback, then anonymous JSON (source of truth) and optional CSV. Answers stay in that browser. Nothing is transmitted automatically.

    How to turn the mode on is documented in that protocol, not as this page’s primary play button. View educator resources ↗(opens in a new tab)

    Run this yourself

    A reproducible workflow for another team. Casual game play does not require steps 4–8. The evaluation workflow is optional for educators and workshops.

    1. Choose the audience (general public, elementary, university, self-paced).
    2. Define the learning objective (Track A decision claims, or Track B olfactory / synbio ideas — not both as one score).
    3. Select the relevant Learning Lab module and/or Odor Pixel game.
    4. If evaluation is intended, administer the pre-instrument (Lab quiz, or game study mode).
    5. Run the activity.
    6. Administer the paired post-instrument.
    7. Match responses using the documented anonymous / item-ID workflow. Paired n counts only sessions with scored pre and post for the same ID. Do not impute missing posts.
    8. Report exact denominators and limitations. Empty is not zero.
    9. Collect qualitative feedback separately from anonymous JSON. Do not merge with class lists.
    10. Change the educational material only if evidence supports a redesign, and log it.

    Limits

    What our evidence does not yet show

    These are the bounds of the claim, not an apology for building the tools.

    • Initial survey sample caveats and misconception counts: Diagnosis (authoritative). Not a population prevalence or learning-gain study.
    • Exact-item pre/post may create familiarity effects. Transfer-item results are not available — see measurement caution.
    • An immediate post-test, when it exists, will not establish long-term retention.
    • Social engagement does not establish learning. Reach figures are not reported yet.
    • Live Odor Pixel Suite / Learning Lab ≠ educational effectiveness. Game 2’s live hub title (Odor Maze) and educator-pack title (QC Shift) currently differ at the same URL.
    • Planned workshop and camp cannot yet provide outcome evidence. Different age groups will not be pooled as one equivalent cohort.
    • Survey and Module 2 share food-safety items; games teach olfactory coding / mixture decoding. We will not report a single correction rate across instruments unless items actually match.
    • This Education wiki page is English. The game suite is bilingual (zh-Hant / English). No separate bilingual teacher packet is published on this wiki.
