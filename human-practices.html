Our starting assumption

We began with a sensing technology. Stakeholders redirected us toward a specific user, decision and standard of evidence.

Our initial concept was a general-purpose electronic nose combining living receptors with lock-in electronics. We considered multiple crops, a lower-cost laboratory instrument and food-safety applications. Interviews challenged all three assumptions: useful odor detection requires a defined workflow and application-specific validation.

=======

The idea we took into the room

We arrived with a technology. The conversations made us ask who would use it, which decision it would support, and what a responsible claim would require.

The project could be told as a general-purpose electronic nose: a living receptor layer plus lock-in electronics that might serve many facility-agriculture crops, compete as a cheaper laboratory instrument, and describe mold VOC detection as food-safety detection. Showing that AeroSense can detect odor — with strong electronics and algorithms — looked like enough to compete.

Values

  • Early enough to act — detect change before it is only visible after loss.
  • Uncertainty must be visible — risk tier + confidence, not a black-box binary.
  • Claims must follow evidence — screening language until validation exists.
  • Modular technology, target-specific claims — a receptor library does not excuse application-first marketing.

  2. Agricultural context

    TY

    Taoyuan District Agricultural Improvement Station — Plant Protection Lab

    Field interview · date not published on this wiki

    Visual inspection is late; VOC early warning is a plausible gap

    What we thought
    A generic multi-crop electronic-nose pitch could stand without a ground-checked agricultural beachhead. Visual inspection and slow lab assays were the detection options we assumed the field already lived with.
    What we heard
    Asymptomatic spread and post-harvest timing make visual inspection late; VOC early warning is a plausible gap versus slow lab assays.
    What changed
    Supported narrowing toward an agricultural disease-detection beachhead rather than a generic multi-crop e-nose pitch.
    Where the change appears
    Entrepreneurship · beachhead (link back to this checkpoint rather than retelling it).
    Evidence / follow-up
    Plant-protection specialists at Taoyuan District Agricultural Improvement Station who work on crop disease problems in the field. Field interview and site visit. Interview reported in the team narrative; no original dated note or photo-permission record is archived. This checkpoint is the field origin of the beachhead story later tightened by FITI written reviews.

  3. User / buyer tension

    FA

    Local farmers’ associations and facility farms

    Completed interviews · exact dates not published on this wiki

    Prioritize institutional buyers

    What we thought
    The individual agricultural grower is the obvious first customer, and a broad facility-agriculture story can stay multi-crop.
    What we heard
    Institutional agricultural channels are more plausible near-term partners than assuming every individual grower is the buyer. Field handling practices and pain points exist, but a broad multi-crop story still read as “technology looking for a use.”
    What changed
    Shifted outreach language toward associations / stations as validation and possible procurement channels; kept field evidence while the commercial beachhead narrowed to a specific expensive problem (banana export disease detection as candidate). Exporters remain a separate gap.
    Where the change appears
    Entrepreneurship · who needs this.
    Evidence / follow-up
    Local agricultural associations organize producers and channel information, practices, and sometimes procurement. Facility / protected-agriculture operators live with disease and quality risk in production. Completed interviews on real-world practices used in economic and outreach framing. Exporters remain uninterviewed — association interviews cannot substitute for exporter willingness-to-pay. No original dated note or site-photo permission record is archived.

  4. 02

    Challenge

  5. Sensor-industry feedback

    EN

    Electronic-nose industry expert (CSO)

    Completed interview · date not published · identity withheld pending confirmation / consent

    Biological recognition is the key challenge

    What we thought
    Showing that AeroSense can detect odor — with strong electronics and algorithms — would be enough to compete. We needed to know what a VOC sensor must do for people who already ship electronic noses — not what makes a demo look good.
    What we heard
    Priorities: selectivity, field-robust sensitivity, lifetime, and reproducibility. Biological recognition is the key challenge.
    What changed
    Reframed AeroSense differentiation around the biological sensing layer and the hardware noise-rejection path needed to protect faint signals. Keep the biological receptor layer as the differentiation bet; require hardware noise-rejection (for example lock-in readout) so faint signals survive real environments.
    Where the change appears
    Hardware READ-layer integrity work; Engineering first-board → current-reader redesign drivers; Design / Parts receptor panel.
    Evidence / follow-up
    Technical-loop interview on sensing-element priorities under real deployment conditions. Company naming pending. Revalidation: staged hardware validation and field-like noise tests — not demo-only traces — remains pending. No original dated note or photo-permission record is archived.

  6. Industry claim boundary

    CB

    Challenge Bioproducts Co., Ltd.

    12 Sep 2026

    VOC signal ≠ food-safety verdict

    What we thought
    Mold VOC detection can be described directly as food-safety detection. Choose an attractive application first (for example mold detection), then find receptor targets that fit it.
    What we heard
    Start from the technology, not the application — establish what AeroSense can actually distinguish before locking a market claim. Mold VOCs do not necessarily represent food safety — processing can kill viable fungi or change their odor profile while a toxin such as aflatoxin remains, so a mold-VOC signal and an actual safety verdict are not the same claim.
    What changed
    Use “VOC risk screening”; avoid definitive safe/unsafe claims; route high-risk samples to confirmatory laboratory testing; report risk tier with visible confidence. Adopted the target-selection framework: Detectability × Consequence × Unmet Need × Willingness to Pay. Separated the agricultural beachhead from a longer-horizon food-safety / pathogen-VOC thread on the same platform.
    Where the change appears
    Responsible implementation; Module 5 screening language; receptor selection evidence.
    Evidence / follow-up
    Completed industry interview (team record; paraphrases on IHP). Individual speaker names are not published here. Formal regulatory / biotech-law advice remains planned. Filling framework cells with measured detectability and buyer evidence remains pending — not literature hypotheses alone.

  7. 03

    Decide

  8. Analytical-instrumentation perspective

    MS

    Analytical-instrumentation / GC–MS–LC–MS professional

    13 Sep 2026 · name withheld pending confirmation

    Screen on site; confirm in the laboratory

    What we thought
    AeroSense could be positioned as a cheaper, faster alternative that stands in for conventional laboratory analytical instruments. The individual agricultural grower is the obvious first customer for a field VOC tool.
    What we heard
    Amortized instrument cost is not the prohibitive barrier assumed; trained personnel and infrastructure are. Individual growers often resist tools that flag their own product. Industry is already moving toward smaller mobile labs. Laboratory assays are already strong at identification; in many workflows their limits are time, destruction, infrastructure, and continuous impracticality — not the absence of analytical capability.
    What changed
    Position AeroSense as rapid, lower-burden, on-site screening before laboratory confirmation — Fast · On-site · Low operator dependence — an early decision-support layer, not a drop-in GC-MS substitute. Buyer segment corrected toward associations, stations, cooperatives, and certification bodies — without claiming any of them as a contracted customer.
    Where the change appears
    Description screening language; SCREEN → TRIAGE → CONFIRM.
    Evidence / follow-up
    Completed industry interview; documented in Engineering Cycle 3 and Entrepreneurship learning cards. Cost figures are interviewee-reported estimates. Operator workflow tests and confirmatory-lab handoff design with real QC actors remain pending. Measured screening performance still pending. Exporter and packing-house interviews on workflow, loss ownership, and willingness to pay remain pending.

Garage+ continues to pressure financial and narrative materials; it does not create a paying customer by itself. No separate one-off pivot is attributed only to Garage+ beyond reinforcing the FITI beachhead direction.

    Entrepreneurship · beachhead; commercial narrative discipline on Entrepreneurship.

Ethical analysis Safety and regulation

Use with care

Responsible implementation

VOC screening can enable earlier triage — and can harm if outputs are over-trusted. Containment detail: Safety & Security. Footprint trade-offs: Sustainability.

Operating principle

Do SCREEN → TRIAGE → CONFIRM

Not SENSE → CERTIFY

AeroSense is an early signal that can prioritize attention. It does not finish a food-safety, toxin, or clinical question.

Learning through exchange

Mentor reviews and public exchanges sharpened our business plan and explanation of the technology. The records below document participation and its effect on our work; they do not establish commercial partnerships or validated applications.

March 2026 · NTHU Garage+

Building the first business plan

Selection into the 18th cohort of NTHU Garage+ gave us a base for mentor office hours and a structured introduction to go-to-market and fundraising questions. Our first business-plan draft took shape here, before external review narrowed the market story.

What this changed. We had to explain who would use the platform and why a new sensing capability would matter in their workflow. That gave the later FITI review something concrete to challenge.

May 2026 · S. Y. Dao Cancer Prevention Foundation

Exploring future applications

We approached the S. Y. Dao Cancer Prevention Foundation to discuss the wider potential of olfactory sensing. The conversation connected our food and nutrition research direction with questions about possible future medical applications.

What this changed. It helped us separate a platform's potential from its present claim. A discussion of medical applications does not establish diagnostic performance; our current project remains a research-stage VOC screening platform.

May 2026 · SynBioBeta

Explaining across disciplines

At the NextGen Bioleader / poster session, we introduced AeroSense to an industry-facing synthetic-biology audience. Visitors could encounter the platform without the background context our own lab takes for granted.

What this changed. It made the connection among the living sensor, optical reader and decoding model a central part of the explanation. The event gave us an opportunity to test the clarity of that story.

August 2026 · NSTC Startup Innovation Bootcamp

Eleven gaps, one revised plan

A written mentor review on August 27 identified eleven gaps in the full business plan. Its central instruction was to begin with market pain and then test whether our strengths actually addressed it. Exchanges with other teams, mentors and judges added practical questions about positioning and communication.

What this changed. We rebuilt the market-sizing argument around the chosen use case and refined the pitch's comparison criteria. Modeled costs, target pricing and future milestones now carry their assumptions alongside the numbers.

September 2026 · China Medical University

Adapting to the audience

We were invited to share AeroSense in a neuroscience course at the home institution of our advisor, Prof. Ching-Che Charng. We introduced synthetic biology, iGEM and the project to students with a different scientific starting point.

What this changed. We practised beginning with the biological logic before connecting it to engineering. The questions after the talk offered another check on which parts of the story were clear.

October 2026 · organised by iGEM TAU

Learning from other teams

We took part in the 2026 iGEM Teams Meetup organised by Tel Aviv University's team. The gathering connected teams from the Americas, Europe and Asia. Hearing different ways to frame a project gave us another opportunity to examine our own assumptions before meeting the community in person.

Next: test the revised decisions

Next, specialists will review our screening claims, operators will review the workflow, and exporters or packing houses will test the commercial assumptions. Each follow-up must record the date, participant role, consented summary, reviewed artifact and resulting decision.

Eleven points that required a rewrite.

A written mentor review on August 27 identified eleven gaps in the full business plan. Its central instruction was to begin with market pain and then test whether our strengths actually addressed it. Exchanges with other teams, mentors and judges added practical questions about positioning and communication.

What this changed. We rebuilt the market-sizing argument around the chosen use case and refined the pitch's comparison criteria. Modeled costs, target pricing and future milestones now carry their assumptions alongside the numbers.

September 2026 · China Medical University

Let the audience change the explanation.

We were invited to share AeroSense in a neuroscience course at the home institution of our advisor, Prof. Ching-Che Charng. We introduced synthetic biology, iGEM and the project to students with a different scientific starting point.

What this changed. We practised beginning with the biological logic before connecting it to engineering. The questions after the talk offered another check on which parts of the story were clear.

October 2026 · organised by iGEM TAU

Compare stories across teams.

We took part in the 2026 iGEM Teams Meetup organised by Tel Aviv University's team. The gathering connected teams from the Americas, Europe and Asia. Hearing different ways to frame a project gave us another opportunity to examine our own assumptions before meeting the community in person.

Future work: close the revalidation loop

The next engagement must test the changed artifact with its intended user: the screening language with analytical and food-safety specialists, the workflow with operators, and the commercial assumptions with exporters or packing houses. A completed follow-up needs a date, participant role, consented summary, the artifact reviewed and a recorded decision to retain or revise it.

The documentary priorities are the original notes and permission records for the six checkpoints above, plus an institutional determination for participant research. These records cannot be recovered by rewriting this page; a new retrospective account must be dated as such.
