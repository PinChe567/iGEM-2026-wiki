Integrated Human Practices · iGEM 2026

How people changed AeroSense.

AeroSense did not move from idea to product in a straight line. Stakeholders challenged who we should build for, what our sensor must measure, what we can responsibly claim, and how a result should lead to action.

Commitments

Our values

Our mission — let machines smell at a biological level, and turn odor into a measurable data dimension — is only worth pursuing if it is built responsibly. Four principles stay visible; expand only for the supporting rationale.

Early enough to act.

A result that arrives after the batch has already spoiled or shipped is not useful, however accurate it is. We optimize for detecting change before it is visible — the premise of a VOC platform.

Uncertainty must be visible.

Industry engineers (CSO interview paraphrase) and respondents in our directional survey (n = 28) distrust a black-box binary verdict more than an imperfect answer with a visible confidence signal. We aim to report a risk tier and confidence — not a false promise of certainty. This is not a population estimate.

Claims must follow evidence.

Mold or disease VOC signals are not automatically a food-safety or diagnostic verdict. Stakeholder feedback pushed us to say what AeroSense can distinguish before marketing what it certifies — and to keep screening language until validation exists.

Modular technology, target-specific claims.

The platform is a programmable receptor library, not a device hard-wired to one target. That flexibility is also a discipline: each claim must be scientifically defensible target by target — rather than assuming the first application (mold detection) was automatically the right one.

Question 01

Is the problem early detection, or simply better detection?

Question / initial assumption

We assumed the gap was “better sensing” in the abstract. Field and food contexts suggested something narrower: damage and spoilage often become chemically visible as VOCs days to weeks before anyone can see them, while today’s options split between fast symptom-only inspection and slow, often destructive lab assays.

Stakeholder input

Taoyuan Plant Protection Lab, farmers’ associations, and facility farms — grounded whether asymptomatic disease timing is a real operational pain. Public survey respondents (Education; directional n = 28) — informed how consumer-side trust and error intolerance feed claim language; see Public survey.

What we learned

  • Visual inspection is late by definition once symptoms appear.
  • Lab assays (PCR / ELISA / GC-MS) are strong analytically but slow, often destructive, and impractical to run continuously across shipments.
  • The missing capability is earlier, non-destructive triage information — not another definitive assay that arrives after the batch has moved.

What changed in AeroSense

Changed because of this
  • Product role: early-screening / decision-support framing rather than “better lab replacement.”
  • Success criterion: useful signal before visible damage — not only higher analytical sophistication.
  • Claim wording: triage language that can escalate to confirmatory testing.

Question 02

What actually limits an electronic nose?

Question / initial assumption

We could have optimized for a compelling odor demo and treated electronics or algorithms as the main competitive story. Before locking engineering priority, we asked people who already ship electronic noses what fails in practice.

Stakeholder input

Electronic-nose industry expert (CSO) — identity withheld pending confirmation — whose perspective mattered because it sets the industry bar for selectivity, field noise, lifetime, and batch reproducibility, not a student demo bar.

What we learned

  • Selectivity is scarce in commercial e-noses; cross-response is the default.
  • Bench sensitivity often collapses in field or warehouse noise.
  • Lifetime and batch-to-batch reproducibility are manufacturing/QA problems as much as chemistry problems.
  • The sensing element — not electronics alone — is where the industry remains stuck.

What changed in AeroSense

Changed because of this
  • Validation plan: clear the sensing-layer bar (selectivity / field SNR / lifetime / reproducibility) before overselling readout cleverness.
  • Differentiation story: compete at the receptor element, not as “another e-nose box.”
  • Hardware requirement: protect faint signals under real noise (continued in Question 05).

Question 03

Why use biology at all?

Question / initial assumption

Given the industry bar, a biological sensing layer is only justified if it is structurally different from another metal-oxide or polymer film — not if it is a novelty wrapper on the same limits.

Stakeholder input

Electronic-nose CSO framing set the problem. Scientific advisors (Lo / Charng lineage) shape the neuromorphic and receptor science we inherit. Institutional biosafety oversight constrains what “living cartridge” actually means in practice. A dedicated cell-handling interview remains an open follow-up in the IHP draft.

What we learned

  • Fixed chemical films are hard to reprogram without physically replacing the sensor.
  • A living OR/Orco + GCaMP reporter layer can, in principle, renew receptor protein and swap receptor identity at the genetic level.
  • That choice creates containment, waste, and eventually technology-transfer duties that inert sensors do not have.

What changed in AeroSense

Changed because of this
  • Architecture bet: keep biology at the sensing element; treat electronics as the readout that must not erase that advantage.
  • SOP / Safety: HEK293T cartridge work stays under contained-use framing — see Safety, not marketing copy.
  • Governance: TTO engagement stays planned until IP pathway is formalized.

See Design · Safety · Safe to deploy · Stakeholders · biosafety

Evidence

Industry interview synthesis; advisor research lineage; institutional biosafety requirements. No claim that biology has already beaten commercial e-noses in head-to-head field trials.

Evidence status

Completed industry input · biosafety Ongoing · cell-ops interview Planned

What we still need to test

  • Operator SOP burden and cartridge waste pathway with a dedicated safety interview.
  • Measured cartridge lifetime and batch drift (do not treat “living renewing receptor” as proven non-aging).
  • Whether receptor swap modularity is practical in the team’s wet-lab timeline — see Parts / Results.

Question 04

Which molecules are actually worth sensing?

Question / initial assumption

Receptor choice could have been arbitrary or marketing-led. We needed an evidence path from literature ligand profiles to claims we are willing to print.

Stakeholder input

Literature: Zboray et al. (2023) heterologous OR ligand profiling toward plant/pathogen volatiles (full citation in References; DOI 10.1038/s41598-023-47455-4). Chia-Nien follow-up: pushed us to map pathogen-indicator VOCs against the receptors we already cloned, and to refuse quiet conflation of fungal vs bacterial markers.

What we learned

  • Seven candidates (Or10a, Or13a, Or47a, Or49b, Or71a, Or85b, Or98a) were sequenced from a fungal/plant-VOC-oriented library — not a blank slate.
  • Or49b has independent literature support for high sensitivity to indole (a classic E. coli-associated volatile) — a hypothesis for later testing, not a product result.
  • Or13a’s primary ligand 1-octen-3-ol is better known as a fungal volatile (“mushroom alcohol”) than as a bacteria-specific marker — so it strengthens mold/food-VOC logic more than a bacterial claim.

What changed in AeroSense

Changed because of this
  • Target choice: keep fungal/plant VOC logic and bacterial exploration as separate threads — do not merge them in claim language.
  • Validation plan: ligand hypotheses must meet wet-lab evidence before Food & Nutrition claims harden.
  • Claim wording: literature support ≠ demonstrated AeroSense performance.

Question 05

Can a weak biological signal survive the real world?

Question / initial assumption

A sensitive receptor is useless if ambient light and electronic noise erase the calcium-fluorescence trace the moment the instrument leaves a quiet bench.

Stakeholder input

CSO interview flagged field SNR collapse as a common failure mode. Hardware design work answers that requirement; vendor/engineer outreach noted in the IHP draft is still incomplete.

What we learned

  • Frequency-division multiplexed excitation plus digital lock-in recovery is the team’s path to pull faint fluorescence out of broadband noise.
  • Classification must run as an edge-capable neuromorphic path grounded in advisor lineage — see Model — without pretending paper results equal product performance.
  • Human Practices standard: honesty under noise matters more than a clean demo trace.

Changed because of this

  • UI / output: if SNR is poor, the instrument should not invent certainty — confidence must reflect measurement quality.

An automated risk score can create harm in two directions: missed real risk (false negative) and over-action or false reassurance (false positive / overclaim). Designing the score without designing the failure mode is incomplete Human Practices.

Ethical analysis Safety and regulation

Use with care

Responsible implementation & impact

This section is not a benefit brochure. It pairs what VOC screening could enable with what can go wrong if outputs are over-trusted — and locks the communication layer to triage, not certification. Detailed containment and laboratory practice live on Safety & Security; footprint trade-offs live on Sustainability.

Operating principle

Do SCREEN → TRIAGE → CONFIRM

Not SENSE → CERTIFY

AeroSense is designed as an early signal that can prioritize attention. It does not finish a food-safety, toxin, or clinical question.

What AeroSense could enable

Conditional aims — none of these is demonstrated as a measured field outcome on this wiki.

  • Earlier intervention — VOC change before visible spoilage or late-stage symptoms, when isolation or rerouting is still possible.
  • Prioritization of samples — route scarce confirmatory-lab capacity toward higher-risk lots first.
  • More frequent screening — non-destructive headspace reads that could be repeated more often than destructive assays (architecture intent; cadence unvalidated).
  • Reduced unnecessary laboratory workload — only if triage is accurate enough that low-tier lots do not flood the lab with false alarms.
  • Potential reduction of food loss — hypothesized earlier isolation of spreading risk; not measured.
  • Broader access to screening — institutional portable readers as a design goal; affordability and access remain unproven and unequal by default.

What could go wrong

Failure modes that Human Practices and Safety treat as design constraints, not edge cases.

  • False negatives → false reassurance — the failure mode directional survey respondents punish hardest (Public survey: 11 / 28 specifically could not accept a missed real risk).
  • False positives → unnecessary disposal / economic loss — over-caution discard of acceptable lots, especially for smallholders.
  • VOC signal ≠ food safety — a mold- or disease-associated odor pattern is not a toxin assay or legal verdict (Trail C; Chia-Nien interview).
  • Biological cartridge containment and disposal — living HEK293T cartridges require sealed handling, inactivation, and waste routes; 2026 scope is laboratory contained-use only (Safety).
  • Cell-batch / environmental variation — biology and ambient conditions can shift baselines; reproducibility is a sensing requirement, not a settled claim.
  • Over-trust in automated scores — opaque “safe / unsafe” labels invite exactly the harm this section forbids.
  • Unequal affordability / access — “portable” language without cost evidence can hide who is left out.
  • Data ownership and cross-partner VOC datasets — multi-partner pilots need governance before datasets scale (proposed; not yet a formal policy).
  • Regulatory uncertainty — device class and pathway are not confirmed by a qualified regulatory source (see below).

Responsible output — conceptual interface

Conceptual UI only. No production operator dashboard is claimed in this repository. Labels show what AeroSense is allowed to say — and what it must never say.

What AeroSense does not claim

  • Not a replacement for confirmatory laboratory analysis.
  • Not a toxin concentration measurement unless separately validated.
  • Not a universal food-safety verdict.
  • Not a pathogen-specific detector based solely on literature association.
  • Not a clinical or medical diagnostic (and not claimed as one in Phase-1 framing).
  • Not a measured reduction in food loss, waste, or laboratory cost.

Regulatory status

Regulatory working hypothesis — confirmation required. IHP notes distinguish general VOC / agricultural screening language from clinical or medical-device pathways as an internal planning distinction. No formal regulatory classification letter, TFDA determination, or completed biotech-law consult is published on this wiki. A dedicated safety interview also remains unfinished in the IHP source draft.

Concrete validation still required

Not a generic “future work” list — these are the measurements and reviews that must exist before stronger claims.

  1. Confusion-cost study with operators: measure decisions after false-negative vs false-positive presentations of the risk-tier UI (monitor / isolate / confirmatory handoff).
  2. Confirmatory-lab handoff trial: time, cost, and acceptance of “isolate → laboratory assay” for at least one institutional workflow.
  3. Cartridge containment package: leak / failure tests, transport and inactivation SOP, disposal route — before any extra-laboratory live-cell use (Safety · containment boundary).
  4. Batch and environment QC ladder: replicate protocol that reports cell-batch and ambient-condition contribution to score drift.
  5. Qualified regulatory determination: written advice that confirms or replaces the Phase-1 agricultural screening working hypothesis.
  6. Data-governance check-in: ownership, access, and retention rules before multi-partner VOC datasets are pooled.
  7. Access / cost evidence: dated hardware BOM and service-cost assumptions before “affordable” or “broader access” language.

4 · What changed

Changed because of this

  • Finding. General mold-danger awareness was already high in this sample; residual gaps sat on heat (4 / 28 did not reject) and look/smell (5 / 28 did not reject).

    Change. Education teaches decision habits (heat / sensory checks), not a generic “mold is dangerous” lecture. See Education.

  • Finding. False-negative intolerance appears more often than false-alarm worry in the published counts (11 / 28 vs 1 / 28), with many unable to accept either error (15 / 28).

    Change. Product communication favors risk tier + confidence + confirmatory escalation — never a silent “safe.” See Trail C · Question 08.

  • Finding. Accuracy and credibility mentions (18 and 17) outnumber price mentions (10) in the published product-requirements synthesis.

    Change. Claim and trust design outrank consumer price storytelling for this sample; we still do not treat mention counts as validated willingness to pay for agricultural buyers.

  • Finding. Date-marking and desiccant use were low relative to refrigeration / sealed storage in this sample.

    Change. Reinforces that household habits are uneven; AeroSense B2B beachhead remains institutional, not a home-gadget story driven by this survey.

5 · Limitations

  • Convenience / non-random sample. No sampling frame supports inference beyond these 28 respondents.
  • Small n. Counts are fragile; single respondents move percentages by ~3.6 points.
  • Population skew. General-consumer-skewed; 45–54 overrepresented (10 / 28). Not “the public.”
  • Incomplete partitions. Risk-tolerance counts sum to 27, not 28.
  • Missing assets. Raw data, full instrument, dates, and chart files are not in this repository; Education already notes survey chart files are absent.
  • Wrong use cases. This survey must not be cited as technical validation, field performance, or exporter willingness to pay.

Canonical Education write-up: education.html#survey · Sustainability verification note on the unverified 100% discard synthesis: sustainability.html

What we still do not know

Continuation roadmap

What we still do not know

Responsible Human Practices includes naming missing evidence and missing voices — not only celebrating interviews that already happened. The items below are unresolved by design: they are neither failures nor completed work. They are the concrete questions future AeroSense work (and future teams) should close before stronger claims.

01 Open technical questions

Questions the sensing stack and claim language still cannot answer with published AeroSense measurements.

Next

What target concentrations are realistically detectable?

Why it matters
Without calibrated detection limits for specific VOC markers, “early enough to act” remains a design intent, not a measured capability.
Who should answer it
Wet-lab assay owners + Hardware / Model teams using defined standards and blank controls.
What evidence would close the question
Concentration–response curves (or limit-of-detection estimates) for named targets under stated assay conditions — published on Results / Experiments, not inferred from literature alone.

See Targets · Results · Experiments

Next

How stable are cell-based cartridges across batches and time?

Why it matters
Industry CSO input ranked reproducibility and lifetime as hard requirements. Batch drift can silently change risk tiers.
Who should answer it
Wet-lab cell-ops + Safety / QC protocol owners.
What evidence would close the question
A replicate protocol that reports cell-batch and time contribution to score drift, plus usable cartridge lifetime under stated storage and read conditions.

See Journey 02 · Responsibility · validation · Safety

Next

How well can related VOC / microbial signatures be distinguished?

Why it matters
The analytical-instrumentation interview framed resolution — not only anomaly detection — as the harder and more valuable problem. Over-claiming “species ID” from an odor pattern would be unsafe.
Who should answer it
Wet lab + Model, with panels of closely related odorants / matrices defined in advance.
What evidence would close the question
Confusion matrices or equivalent discrimination metrics on a locked panel; explicit statement of what cannot be resolved.

See Engagement · analytical · Model · Engineering

In progress

How robust is the reader outside controlled laboratory conditions?

Why it matters
Screening value depends on usable signal in noise, vibration, temperature, and ambient odor — not only on a bench demo.
Who should answer it
Hardware team executing the published validation ladder (dark noise → calibration → FDM/DLIA → biological fluorescence).
What evidence would close the question
Dated hardware validation records under stated environmental conditions before any packing-house or field performance claim.

See Journey 05 · Hardware

Blocked / needs partner

What false-negative rate is acceptable for each use case?

Why it matters
Directional survey respondents punish missed real risk hardest, but an acceptable FN rate is a use-case and liability decision — not a lab metric alone.
Who should answer it
Intended institutional operators, confirmatory-lab partners, and (for the beachhead) exporters / QC decision-makers — with Safety / HP facilitating.
What evidence would close the question
Documented acceptance thresholds per workflow (screen → triage → confirm), plus an operator confusion-cost study of risk-tier UI responses.

See Public survey · Responsibility · Journey 08

02 Missing stakeholder / deployment evidence

Voices and real-world tests that institutional interviews and mentorship cannot substitute for.

Blocked / needs partner

Direct buyer / exporter validation

Why it matters
The banana-export beachhead is still a candidate narrative. Agricultural-station and association interviews cannot prove exporter willingness to adopt or pay.
Who should answer it
Banana / mango exporters and packing-house decision-makers (mapped as planned stakeholders).
What evidence would close the question
Completed interviews (or structured site visits) documenting problem cost, decision rights, and whether a VOC screen would change ship / hold / test behavior.

See Stakeholders · exporters · Trail E · Entrepreneurship

Blocked / needs partner

Packing-house workflow observation

Why it matters
Without watching time, space, staffing, and QC handoffs, “on-site screening” can be incompatible with real operations even if the sensor works on a bench.
Who should answer it
Packing-house operations / QC staff, with team observers under agreed access.
What evidence would close the question
A dated workflow map: where a read would fit, who acts on a Medium/High tier, and what breaks if a retest is required.

See Trail B · Journey 07

Next

Regulatory classification

Why it matters
Phase-1 agricultural VOC screening vs clinical pathways is only an internal working hypothesis until a qualified source confirms or replaces it.
Who should answer it
Qualified regulatory / biotech-law advisor (planned engagement; not completed).
What evidence would close the question
Written advice that states device class, pathway assumptions, and prohibited claim language for the intended use.

See Responsibility · regulatory · Stakeholders · regulatory advice

In progress

Biosafety / cartridge disposal consultation

Why it matters
Living HEK293T cartridges create containment and waste duties beyond inert sensors. Extra-laboratory use is outside iGEM 2026 scope until validated.
Who should answer it
Institutional biosafety oversight + a dedicated safety / cell-ops interview (still unfinished in the IHP draft).
What evidence would close the question
Leak / failure tests, inactivation and disposal SOP, and explicit go/no-go criteria for any future partner-site cartridge use.

See Safety · Safe to deploy · Stakeholders · biosafety · Responsibility

Blocked / needs partner

Willingness-to-pay validation

Why it matters
Consumer survey mention counts and an unpublished NT$150 percentage are not B2B willingness-to-pay evidence for agricultural buyers.
Who should answer it
Plausible payers (associations, stations, exporters, certifiers) — not general-consumer respondents.
What evidence would close the question
Buyer interviews or procurement scenarios with stated price bands, budget owners, and non-adoption reasons.

See Public survey · Entrepreneurship

Next

Confirmatory-lab workflow

Why it matters
SCREEN → TRIAGE → CONFIRM fails if the confirmatory handoff is too slow, costly, or socially rejected by operators.
Who should answer it
Testing-service / confirmatory-lab partners and the institutional operator who would escalate High / invalid reads.
What evidence would close the question
A timed handoff trial: sample type, assay chosen, turnaround, cost, and acceptance of “isolate → lab assay” as SOP.

See Responsibility · Analytical interview

Blocked / needs partner

Field pilot

Why it matters
No measured food-loss reduction or field detection performance is claimed from Human Practices alone — and none should be until containment, hardware, and partner access allow a real pilot.
Who should answer it
A willing institutional host (station, packing house, or association) plus Hardware / Wet lab under Safety constraints.
What evidence would close the question
A scoped pilot protocol with n, success criteria, confirmatory comparison, and explicit non-claims — only after laboratory validation and containment package.

See Safety · containment boundary · Journey 01

Next

IP / technology-transfer ownership clarity

Why it matters
Receptor and neuromorphic-layer IP originates in institute research; partnering and naming claims need ownership clarity.
Who should answer it
NTHU Technology Transfer Office (planned — not yet formally engaged on this wiki).
What evidence would close the question
Documented TTO engagement outcome on ownership, licensing path, and what the team may publicly claim.

See Stakeholders · TTO

Documentation gaps that are not research questions (survey raw file, engagement photos, CSO company naming pending confirmation) remain listed on Public survey and Engagements rather than upgraded into “solved” Human Practices.

Closing

Conclusion

Human Practices changed AeroSense by forcing evolution: narrower problems, harder sensing criteria, and stricter claim language. The work is not finished — open interviews and formal advice still sit ahead — but the project no longer presents a day-one story as if it were the only story.

The open roadmap is public on purpose: What we still do not know. Next priorities include exporter engagement, regulatory and safety consults, and publishing additional Decision Trail cases only when consent and notes allow.
