Project Description

AeroSense is a planned bio-digital platform that pairs living-cell olfactory receptors with fly-inspired computation and portable optical readout to interpret volatile organic compound patterns.

Project scope and system rationale

The challenge

Patterns of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in air can carry information about plants, environments, foods, and biological states. Distinguishing those patterns is hard when many compounds co-occur, concentrations vary, and chemically related molecules produce overlapping sensor responses.

Conventional electronic-nose systems combine chemical gas sensors with signal processing and pattern recognition. Reviews of this field emphasize that gas sensors commonly show partially overlapping selectivity, limited sensitivity, imperfect stability and reproducibility, and substantial noise, which complicates mixture analysis and open-environment use [1] Liu et al., 2023 — full entry in References & Credits. . Baseline and time-varying drift—from aging, poisoning, humidity, temperature, and instrument variation—remain major obstacles to reliable long-term operation [2] Zhai et al., 2024 — full entry in References & Credits. .

These constraints matter for any serious sensing goal: sensitivity sets which signals can be detected; selectivity and mixture handling determine whether similar odors can be separated; drift control determines whether a trained model remains usable over time; and field deployment adds uncontrolled backgrounds, power and maintenance limits, and calibration burden [1] Liu et al., 2023 — full entry in References & Credits. . Electronic noses have already been explored across agriculture, environmental monitoring, food assessment, biomedical research, and other domains [3] Wilson & Baietto, 2009 — full entry in References & Credits. . AeroSense does not claim that all conventional systems fail; it asks whether a living-cell receptor array plus fly-inspired decoding can address mixture discrimination and stability questions that remain difficult for many sensor arrays.

Why learn from olfaction?

Insect odor detection relies on a large family of olfactory receptors (ORs) that partner with a conserved odorant receptor co-receptor (Orco) to form ligand-gated cation channels. In Drosophila melanogaster, genome analyses defined a substantial OR repertoire within the insect chemoreceptor superfamily [4] Robertson et al., 2003 — full entry in References & Credits. . Structural work on Orco shows a tetrameric channel architecture that helps explain how a conserved co-receptor can combine with divergent ORs while supporting ion flux [5] Butterwick et al., 2018 — full entry in References & Credits. .

Downstream of receptors, fly olfactory circuits transform overlapping sensory patterns into more separable codes. Early processing in the antennal lobe (AL) can emphasize contrast among channels, while the mushroom body (MB) uses sparse, high-dimensional representations. Computational analyses of this pathway show that fly-like sparse projection and winner-take-all selection implement a locality-sensitive hashing strategy useful for similarity search [6] Dasgupta et al., 2017 — full entry in References & Credits. . AeroSense treats these ideas as design inspiration for engineered sensors and algorithms, not as a claim that the team has reproduced an insect brain.

The AeroSense concept

AeroSense is designed as a three-layer research platform. Selected Drosophila ORs with Orco are planned for heterologous expression in HEK293T cells; receptor activation is intended to be read as calcium-dependent GCaMP6 fluorescence; those response vectors are planned to enter AL-inspired normalization/inhibition and MB-inspired sparse projection before classification; and a portable optical reader is planned to acquire fluorescence for edge preprocessing and analysis.

Prior published work has shown that insect ORs can be expressed with Orco and GCaMP6 in mammalian cell lines for odorant profiling [7] Zboray et al., 2023 — full entry in References & Credits. . AeroSense builds on that literature as a starting point for its own constructs, assays, models, and hardware. The sections below state design intent. They are not reported AeroSense results.

Candidate application domains discussed in the team proposal include agricultural, environmental, food, and medical VOC contexts. The team still needs to designate a primary 2026 use case before final wiki publication so that receptor choice, datasets, and success criteria stay aligned.

Biological sensing layer

The biological track proposes to co-express selected Drosophila ORs with Orco in HEK293T cells and to monitor calcium-dependent GCaMP6 fluorescence after odorant or agonist exposure. This approach follows published heterologous insect-OR panels that used HEK293 cells, Orco, and GCaMP6 for VOC profiling [7] Zboray et al., 2023 — full entry in References & Credits. . AeroSense plans its own construct set, transfection workflow, and controls; it does not claim that stable AeroSense cell lines or optimized receptor mutants already exist.

As a functional-control stimulus, the team plans to use VUAA1, an Orco-family agonist identified for insect odorant receptor ion channels [8] Jones et al., 2011 — full entry in References & Credits. . Later structural and mutagenesis work has mapped aspects of VUAA1 binding and translocation in Orco [9] Pacalon et al., 2023 — full entry in References & Credits. . Receptor selection and any affinity optimization (for example, structure-guided mutagenesis toward target VOCs) are investigation goals, not completed AeroSense deliverables.

Optional supporting elements under consideration include odorant-binding protein expression and microfluidic or multi-well delivery formats. Those remain design options until experiments define what is necessary for a reproducible assay. Construct details belong on Design; protocols belong on Experiments.

Computational layer

The computational track is designed to convert receptor fluorescence time series into fixed response vectors, then to transform those vectors with AL-inspired normalization and inhibition and MB-inspired sparse projection before a downstream classifier. The fly olfactory circuit’s sparse expansion and winner-take-all selection provide the conceptual basis for locality-sensitive representations of similar odor patterns [6] Dasgupta et al., 2017 — full entry in References & Credits. .

In practice, AeroSense plans to extract features such as relative fluorescence change from each receptor channel, apply contrast-enhancing preprocessing inspired by AL computation, project into a sparse high-dimensional code inspired by Kenyon-cell connectivity, and evaluate classification against simpler baselines on documented datasets. Equations, hyperparameters, training splits, and benchmark tables belong on the Model page. No AeroSense accuracy threshold is stated here as an achieved result.

Hardware layer

The hardware track proposes a portable optical path matched to GCaMP-class fluorescence: excitation illumination, emission filtering to reject excitation bleed-through, photodiode detection, current-to-voltage conversion with a transimpedance amplifier (TIA), digitization with an analog-to-digital converter (ADC), multiplexed multi-channel acquisition, and an edge-computing interface for local preprocessing and transfer to analysis software.

Exact microcontroller, single-board computer, filter optical density, channel count, and enclosure choices are still being reconciled across hardware drafts. This page therefore keeps component names generic. Architecture, bill of materials, and bench validation belong on Hardware. No claim is made here that a field-ready instrument already exists.

Intended context and boundaries

For 2026, AeroSense is a research prototype: a documented attempt to connect living-cell sensing, interpretable computation, and portable optical acquisition under iGEM constraints. Possible future applications in agriculture, environmental monitoring, food assessment, or medical VOC research remain candidate domains until the team selects a primary use case and supplies matching evidence.

  • Research prototype: construct, assay, model, and hardware development with explicit planned/in-progress/validated labels.
  • Possible future applications: domain-specific VOC pattern tasks if receptor panels, datasets, and validation later support them.
  • What AeroSense is not (unless later evidence is published): a clinical diagnostic; a validated food-safety instrument; or a field-certified environmental device.

No sensitivity, specificity, disease-detection, or deployment claim should be read into this page beyond the sourced literature and the planned work described above.

Project goals for 2026

The following are goals, not results.

  1. Build and sequence the planned OR / Orco / GCaMP6 construct set and demonstrate expression in HEK293T cells.
  2. Establish VUAA1 and negative-control assays that support functional interpretation of fluorescence changes.
  3. Collect documented VOC or agonist response datasets suitable for computational evaluation.
  4. Specify and evaluate AL-inspired and MB-inspired model stages against simpler baselines.
  5. Prototype a portable optical acquisition path and characterize noise, filtering, and channel isolation on the bench.
  6. Integrate at least one wet-lab assay path with model analysis and hardware readout documentation.
  7. Record stakeholder input that changes design, safety framing, or claimed use boundaries.

What would count as success?

Success is defined by evidence categories, not by invented numeric thresholds on this page.

  • Functional receptor response. OR/Orco-dependent fluorescence changes under agonist or VOC exposure, with controls that rule out reporter-only artifacts.
  • Reproducibility and controls. Repeated measurements, vehicle and genetic controls, and transparent exclusion criteria.
  • Interpretable model validation. Provenance-labeled datasets, baseline comparisons, and an explanation of what each computational stage is intended to do.
  • Working hardware signal acquisition. Bench data showing that the optical and electronic path can record calibration or assay-relevant signals.
  • Integration. A documented path from a biological measurement through computation and hardware interfaces, even if partial.
  • Responsible stakeholder-informed design. Human Practices evidence that use-case boundaries, safety, or experimental priorities changed because of engagement.

Limitations and open questions

  • Receptor specificity. Selected ORs may respond to multiple ligands; panel choice and cross-reactivity mapping remain open.
  • Mixed-VOC complexity. Laboratory single-compound or simplified mixtures may not represent field backgrounds.
  • Living-cell maintenance. Temperature, medium, viability, and containment constraints limit continuous portable operation.
  • Optical cross-talk. Excitation leakage, channel optical isolation, and multipath fluorescence can distort multi-well signals.
  • Dataset scale. Model claims will be bounded by how many conditions, replicates, and compounds are actually measured.
  • Domain shift. Models trained under one delivery condition may degrade under new humidity, temperature, or background VOC regimes.
  • Safety and containment. HEK293T work and any field-facing enclosure must stay within approved biosafety and waste procedures.
  • Deployment assumptions. Edge connectivity, calibration frequency, and user training are design assumptions until demonstrated.
