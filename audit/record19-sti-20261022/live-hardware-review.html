Dry Lab · Hardware

Hardware

Building a reader around light, airflow and living samples

  1. Home
  2. Dry Lab
  3. Hardware

Abstract

AeroSense combines four replaceable 100 µL wells, frequency-tagged blue LEDs, a filtered photodiode, ESP32 acquisition and a local phone interface. An eight-part enclosure fixes the optical geometry and separates sample gas from electronics cooling. Optical and airflow simulations guided filter selection and the inlet shelf; assembly feedback informed the manual. Fluorescein measurements resolve four frequency channels, quantify cross-well responses and test calibration under isolated and simultaneous loading.

Introduction

Monitoring volatile changes around stored produce requires a practical link between receptor biology and an interpretable measurement. Our sensing strategy pairs insect odorant receptors with the calcium indicator GCaMP6f so that receptor activation can be read optically. [13] [14] The hardware task is to expose several cell samples to the same gas input and record their optical responses in a compact reader with replaceable wells.

A difference between channels should reflect the samples being compared, rather than differences in gas delivery or light collection. We therefore need to control the gas path, keep each well in a fixed position and limit excitation light reaching the detector. Using one photodiode simplifies the detector layout, but requires distinct LED modulation frequencies and digital lock-in processing to recover the excitation channels. These requirements connect the enclosure, optics, electronics and acquisition software.

The exploded view below shows how the gas lid, wells, optical path and electronics fit together. The following sections connect each part to its design question and supporting figures, then examine cell recovery, system verification and the manuals revised after assembly feedback.

Airflow

All four wells share one gas input, so a difference in exposure could be confused with a difference in receptor response. We compared the open lid, perforated plate and integral shelf to ask how each directs the inlet jet and delivers gas toward the liquid surfaces.

The printed gas lid carries an inlet and outlet on opposite sides of one shared chamber. Its integral shelf redirects the incoming stream above the four well openings. A removable compression plate presses the sealing stack around the wells, while flexible couplers connect the gas ports to tubing through the side openings.

Inlet filter→Gas lid and shelf→Shared well headspace→Outlet filter→Controlled suction

In the video, first compare jet direction and speed across the three geometries, then follow the concentration front as the shelf flow rate changes. The arrival-time calculation uses concentration beside each liquid surface, connecting the flow pattern to sample exposure.

At 3 mL/min with 100 µL in each well, the model predicts gas concentration beside the liquid surface reaching 10% of the inlet concentration in 5.30–6.29 s and 90% in 47.80–49.42 s.

At matched conditions (3 mL/min, 100 µL/well and D = 10 mm²/s), the open chamber, 12-hole plate and current shelf had normalized downward-momentum proxies of 1.00, 0.57 and 0.69. Their slowest-well t90 values were 51.67, 48.36 and 49.42 s, with interwell spreads of 4.64, 2.83 and 1.62 s, respectively. We retained the shelf: it reduced the momentum proxy by 31% and brought all four wells to t90 within approximately 50 s, meeting our provisional 60 s gas-transport target. Reusing the completed integrated enclosure also avoided another fabrication cycle. The plate reduced momentum more and remains a candidate for the next version.

Here, t90 is the time for mean gas concentration beside each liquid surface to reach 90% of the inlet value; it excludes external tubing and cellular response. Similar velocities do not guarantee matched concentration arrival, because diffusion also affects transport. The fixed-surface model describes gas delivery beside the medium. A coupled fluid–fluid model would need to solve both phases and their interfacial force balance, as described by COMSOL [24].

Optics

Well illumination

Our design uses one photodiode to read four wells, with LED modulation distinguishing the excitation channels. Light can still reach neighboring wells, so a frequency channel may contain fluorescence from more than its intended sample. The optical model therefore asks how well the geometry preserves each well’s contribution.

In the transfer matrix below, compare the diagonal contributions from the intended well with off-diagonal contributions from neighboring wells. Detailed simulations and the optical-path animation are presented in Model →.

The intended well contributes 88.6–90.9% of each modeled readout. The same matrix is used in Model and in verification Figure 15, which compares the displayed response distributions with measured single-well controls. These percentages describe response distribution, not a fraction of all emitted fluorescence.

Filter selection and spectral alignment

The photodiode responds to both blue excitation and green reporter light. Filter selection therefore balances rejection of excitation leakage against retention of the signal band. The comparison below holds the source and geometry assumptions fixed: read the LED-derived and proxy-green currents separately, then compare the resulting raw peak.

A single LEE 101 layer retains 70.43% of proxy-green current while reducing LED-derived current to 2.987% of the unfiltered model. The filter sits over the collection aperture, leaving the excitation path open.

The filter comparison depends on where excitation, emission and detector sensitivity overlap. In the first panel, compare the LED spectrum with GCaMP6f excitation and locate its green emission. In the second, compare that emission band with filter transmission and photodiode responsivity to follow the collection path.

Electronics

Signal and control

To compare optical channels, we must convert photocurrent into a timed electrical record. The diagram traces the main readout path and distinguishes the LED-control and power branches.

Schematic

Photodiode → TIA → ADC → ESP32

Four independently driven LEDs and one shared photodiode reduce the number of detector channels while preserving a route to frequency-resolved readout. This choice places the burden on stable acquisition and measured optical calibration.

The acquisition specification uses a 4 kHz timebase, two-second I/Q blocks and 41/67/89/113 Hz LED channels.

The acquisition retains in-phase and quadrature components at each excitation frequency, following Scofield’s frequency-domain treatment of lock-in detection. [1]

Keep cooling separate from sample gas

A fan below the PCB cools the electronics separately from the sample headspace supplied by the gas lid. Separating these paths keeps fan speed from becoming a sample-delivery variable; supplementary CAD views show the fan cradle and service openings.

Mechanical drawings and assembly checks

Interface and firmware

One local interface runs measurements, displays channel signals and saves named records. History reopens earlier results; JSON and CSV exports retain the acquisition settings and raw data.

  1. Connect

    Connect to the reader locally and confirm the active data source. The same interface supports device acquisition and a clearly labeled demonstration mode.

  2. Acquire

    Name the sample and choose a single measurement or scheduled acquisition. Advanced settings control block count, interval, LED duty and active LEDs. Each block records two seconds; stopping turns the LEDs off.

  3. Inspect

    Read the sample count, ADC voltage, channel signals and quality flags together. ADC diagnostics compare hardware SPI with slow GPIO sampling and test the data-line response to weak pulls.

  4. Reopen and export

    Open a selected history record, reload JSON or CSV, and export named files. Browser storage retains available raw samples for later inspection.

Reference-pattern analysis

The reader interface sends saved acquisition and calibration records to a local Python backend. For reference analysis, D1–D4 hold Or22a, Or35a, Or85b and Or98a, respectively; the backend reconstructs their ΔF/F₀ curves, averages all four over the same selected response window and returns separate ranked matches against laboratory and DoOR references. The bars show 100 × max(0, Pearson r), a pattern-similarity score rather than an odor probability or validated identification.

Cell viability

A living-cell cartridge needs recoverable viable cells as well as suitable optical geometry. We examined cells recovered from the medium and well bottom after the same incubation. Panel A compares viability in these paired fractions; Panel B converts bottom counts to nominal live and dead recovery density. Read the individual well points alongside the mean and spread.

After 7 h in the printed wells, recovered HEK293–GCaMP6f cells showed 60.0 ± 7.5% viability in the medium and 25.5 ± 0.9% in the bottom recovery fraction (mean ± SD, three paired wells).

The estimated live bottom recovery was 49,667 ± 4,805 cells per well. Recovery estimates use nominal volume and area; bottom recovery can include settled cells and does not measure attachment.

Verification

System verification

Verification follows the measured signal from frequency separation through cross-talk comparison, multi-well additivity and concentration checks. Raw records, calibration calculations and residuals link each result to its acquisition conditions.

All wells contained 100 µL. The study includes uniform standards spanning 0–20 µM, five unequal-well loadings and four isolated-well controls. All 58 acquisitions are retained, including both 2.5 µM loadings examined in quality control.

1. Separate the four frequency-tagged signals

The LEDs label D1–D4 at 41, 67, 89 and 113 Hz. Digital lock-in amplification (DLIA) extracts their in-phase and quadrature components from one photodiode waveform. A fresh illuminated-water blank is subtracted once in complex I/Q space; the corresponding single-well control sets each channel’s phase.

2. Compare simulated and measured cross-talk

A is the optical design simulation and B is the measured single-well response. Columns identify the fluorescent source well; rows identify the LED-frequency/readout channel. C pairs the same 16 matrix positions, with A on the x-axis and B on the y-axis.

The overall Pearson correlation is r = 0.810 across all 16 corresponding matrix entries; the 12 off-diagonal cross-talk entries give r = 0.257.

D4 has the largest own-well share in both matrices: 90.9% simulated and 69.5% measured. Its strongest non-own source is D3 in both: 8.6% and 30.6%. This means source D3 contributes to readout D4. The reverse direction is different: source D4 contributes 8.5% to readout D3 in the simulation but only 0.3% in the measurement.

These comparisons describe response patterns. Section 3 checks a separate requirement for cross-talk correction: whether four isolated-well responses can be added to predict a simultaneous four-well measurement.

3. Multi-well signal additivity

The previous figure shows which source wells affect each readout channel. To subtract those contributions, we first need to check whether the signals measured one well at a time can predict the signal when all four wells contain fluorescein.

We added the four separate single-well measurements to form a prediction. We then measured all four wells together, each at 10 µM. In the chart, each channel’s prediction is set to 100%; the bar is the actual simultaneous measurement. If the signals add up as assumed, the bar should reach the dashed line.

All four measured responses fall below the sum of the isolated-well predictions; D3 reaches only 48.3%. This comparison tests the additivity required to use isolated-well responses for simultaneous loading.

4. Check calibrated concentrations against prepared standards

The figure shows per-channel curve calibration after blank subtraction and phase projection, using the same color and symbol for each channel. Unequal-well loadings were excluded from calibration; uniform-concentration checks exclude all readings at the tested concentration. This analysis does not explicitly invert the cross-well mixing matrix.

The checks compare calibrated estimates with prepared concentrations across loading patterns. Deviations remain at low concentrations and in unequal-well loadings.

Living-cell integration

We followed four cell-containing wells before and after adding VUAA1 (1:100 dilution).

D1 contained Or35a + ORCO, D2 Or85b + ORCO, D3 Or98a + ORCO, and D4 ORCO only.

What changes during the readout?

D2 and D3 increase relative to their first post-addition readings, D4 rises modestly after a brief dip, and D1 decreases; the cells-plus-medium series change only slightly. D3’s large ratio depends on its low first post-addition reference.

The final absolute signals remain 7.5%, 6.1%, 5.6% and 2.7% below the initial three-reading means for D1–D4, respectively.

Comparison with wet-lab kinetics

We compared the VUAA1 trajectories with the corresponding Test 2 wells, using each acquisition’s first reading as its own reference. D2 shows the clearest shared rise, while D1 changes in the opposite direction.

For D1–D4, the matched Pearson correlations are −0.859, +0.972, +0.961 and +0.948; Spearman correlations are −0.80, +0.90, +0.90 and +0.90. The corresponding first-difference correlations are −0.529, +0.650, +0.761 and −0.125. D2 and D3 share positive level and step-to-step trends with their matched wet-lab traces; D1 trends in the opposite direction, while D4 shares less step-to-step variation than its level correlation suggests.

Discussion

The assembled reader combines replaceable cell wells, a shared gas chamber and frequency-tagged optical readout in a serviceable enclosure. Fixed optical registration supports repeated calibration, while separate cooling and sample-gas paths avoid using fan operation to set sample delivery. The tested acquisition workflow and revised assembly manual provide a practical starting point for replication.

The reader separates four modulation channels, but its isolated-well calibration does not directly transfer to simultaneous loading: uniform 10 µM responses are only 48.3–69.4% of the predicted sum. Likewise, strong same-well responses drive much of the simulation–measurement correlation (r = 0.810); the 12 off-diagonal entries give r = 0.257. The floor-layer simulation and liquid-volume standard have different source conditions, and the measured signal includes electronics. Arbitrary-mixture recovery therefore needs matched single-source and mixed-source controls across concentrations.

Viable cells were recovered from printed wells, but the lower viability in the bottom fraction cannot be assigned to resin, culture conditions or recovery without matched controls. Resin choice and post-processing can affect compatibility [21]. Machined PMMA and poly-D-lysine are candidates supported by separate HEK293 studies [22][23]; comparison with standard culture plastic should measure viability, retained cells, optical background and reporter response under matched conditions.

Airflow calculations describe gas delivery beside fixed liquid surfaces, not cell forces or uniform cellular exposure. Nominal CAD dimensions still require fit and alignment checks. An independent timed assembly and operation trial should test the revised manual, recording assistance and errors.

The single cell run does not separate receptor responses from liquid addition or optical mixing; the fluorescein calibration was not applied to it. Its replacement first VUAA1 record matches ADC values in an earlier file described as synthetic, while the archived firmware export differs. The supplied remeasurement description does not resolve that conflict, so these traces remain descriptive evidence of the supplied records.

Cross-platform similarity is not specific to matched constructs: D2 gives r = 0.9719 with Or85b and 0.9671 with Or98a, and D3 exceeds its strongest off-diagonal match by only 0.0055. D2 and D3 remain positive after omitting the first point (r = 0.970 and 0.904), whereas restoring original record 19 changes D4 from +0.948 to −0.290. The original series spans 69.186 s, while the replacement lacks a verified timestamp. Control subtraction preserves some shared trends, but preceding hardware-medium readings and separate wet-lab DMSO wells are different references. Serial time points do not establish synchronized kinetics, biological replication or receptor-specific activation.

Conclusion

AeroSense integrates removable wells, filtered optical collection, shared-photodiode electronics and a local phone interface. Fluorescein measurements demonstrate four-frequency separation and quantify source mixing. The measured I/Q matrix, concentration checks and cell-containing-well traces provide a reproducible basis for selecting the next calibration and biological-control experiments.

Verification protocol

The protocol organizes electrical, optical, transport and workflow verification. Current records include both fluorescein studies, single-well controls, concentration-level holdouts and an assembly-feedback record.

Next calibration sequence

The future developer pilot begins with electrical-rail and ADC-headroom checks, an LED-off baseline (DARK), stop-control checks and observed D1–D4 mapping. Its sodium-fluorescein sequence in PBS is: illuminated PBS calibration blank → independent PBS check blank → four single-well 100 nM conditions (PBS in the other wells) → all wells at 50 nM → all wells at 100 nM → repeat all wells at 50 nM. This sequence contains nine illuminated analytical conditions, each with at least five distinct valid two-second technical blocks; the LED-off baseline is saved separately.

This 50/100 nM PBS pilot is a separate future developer study from the water-based fluorescein calibration reported above. Its approximately five-second record spacing calls for a separate timing assessment before recording fast GCaMP6f cell responses.

Measured matrix correction and its limits

For z = 1000(I + iQ) mV, subtract the same-set DDW mean. Each source column is the corresponding isolated 10 µM net response divided by 10; stack its four I rows and four Q rows into M. Solve ĉ = (MᵀM + λσmax²I)⁻¹Mᵀy with λ = 0.0001, selected from 15 single-source/blank technical leave-one-acquisition-out folds. Matrix condition number: 10.613. No uniform target concentration or mixed-loading truth is used to tune λ.

The measured all-well 10 µM responses are only 68.4%, 69.4%, 48.3% and 65.6% of the summed isolated-source predictions. A fixed additive source matrix must satisfy that sum at its own calibration concentration regardless of the later scalar curve family. Nonnegative constraints reduce technical-control error but set failed components to the boundary; they do not demonstrate correct concentration recovery.

Set A has no independent four-source matrix. Its two unequal concentration patterns and uniform standards span only three source-design directions, so an unrestricted 4-source matrix is not identifiable there. A per-channel complex affine map is fitted to the three shared standard means (0, 1, 10 µM), then the set-B matrix is applied without tuning against mixed-loading truth. Set-B uniform 1/5/10 µM loadings are absent from matrix training; isolated 10 µM controls remain, so these are loading holdouts rather than exclusion of every 10 µM record. Technical repeats, channels and violin density are not independent biological replicates.

Each isolated-source control contains 100 µL of 10 µM fluorescein in one well and 100 µL DDW in each of the other three. All-well standards also contain 100 µL per well. The observed discrepancy therefore occurs with neighboring wells filled in both configurations; these measurements do not isolate its physical cause.

Per-channel curves and quality checks

Figure 17 fits each readout separately without explicitly inverting a cross-well matrix. Its descriptive R² of 0.886 describes the per-channel calibration display. The matrix-derived concentration estimates are reported separately below.

The preceding display used separate calibrations and had not aligned all three shared standards. This revision reconstructs the raw I/Q records, applies the same current-blank and signed-phase processing to both calibration sets, and compares the candidate functions using whole-concentration holdouts.

DDW, 10C and 100C correspond to 0, 1 and 10 µM. All three shared levels were audited. After blank subtraction, the 1-to-10 µM response ratio differs between calibration sets: for D4 it is 0.86% versus 16.18%, so one gain factor cannot align both. A flexible curve can be made to pass through three points, but those same points cannot independently validate it. The main result uses fresh within-set blank correction and the selected inverse curve to obtain common concentration units; no common three-point signal warp is applied.

A joint exponential shape with a separate offset/gain for each calibration set gives an interpolation RMSE of 1.036 µM, but only 19/20 inverses remain in range. The retained functions give 1.043 µM and 20/20 in-range estimates. That small RMSE difference is not a demonstrated statistical advantage. Entire concentrations are removed from both sets and transfer/phase fitting in the joint checks; endpoint extrapolation is reported separately.

Use z = 1000(I + iQ) mV, subtract the current calibration set's DDW mean, then take y = Re[(z − blank) conjugate(phase)]. Set A uses its highest retained training standard as the phase reference; set B uses separate isolated-source controls. The linear form is y = a + b(c/10). The exponential form is y = a + b expm1(kc/20)/expm1(k). All channel coefficients, raw hashes, folds and inverse-validity flags are downloadable.

The original 2.5 µM loading is unstable in D3; the additional loading predates a matching blank. Both remain in the archive and sensitivity analyses. Calibration anchors are not presented as new holdout predictions. Selection and QC are exploratory, channels in one loading are dependent, and signed phase projection does not establish arbitrary-mixture interference removal.

The 0–10 µM set contains 12 independently prepared uniform standards and five withheld unequal-well loadings. The 0–20 µM set supplies three tested interior levels, each averaged over three technical reads. Main-figure means therefore combine 20 withheld mixed-loading estimates with 12 concentration-holdout estimates. Their combined R² and RMSE describe this display, not one independent accuracy specification. Low-concentration evidence remains limited to two held standard levels in the first calibration set.

A historical sensitivity check compared extra residual corrections after the exponential calibration: nested whole-level RMSE was 1.28 µM without an added correction, 1.34 µM with a constant offset and 19.57 µM with a linear adjustment. That extra adjustment is not part of the retained method; the original diagnostic is preserved.

Test interference removal before trusting it

The isolated 10 µM controls supply a measured I/Q response matrix. Leaving out one read from every source and the blank identifies the active well in 12/12 technical checks, with concentration RMSE 0.51 µM. Applying that same matrix to simultaneous equal loading produces a negative D3 estimate; ridge regularization chosen from source-only holdouts does not remove the mismatch. The matrix therefore supports the single-well check, while uniform concentration conversion uses the measured uniform-standard curves.

Compare both 2.5 µM loadings

The additional loading is more repeatable: D1–D4 raw-amplitude CVs are 0.20%, 0.18%, 0.29% and 0.42%. The original D3 readings jump by about 81% between the first and second read. However, the additional loading was acquired earlier and its D3/D4 signals fall below the later water blank, so it lacks a matching baseline. Both loadings remain in the archive and sensitivity analyses; neither enters the primary fit.

Complete raw data, calculations and reproducible scripts

Concentration estimates after matrix unmixing

These diagnostic estimates explicitly apply the measured I/Q mixing matrix. They are 10 µM-reference-equivalent values, not the per-channel curve estimates in Figure 17. The full signed results are retained here.

Mean residuals are +0.03, −0.84, −4.41 and −0.02 µM for D1–D4. A mean near zero does not remove the wide spread. The negative D3 estimates and poor combined fit show that this fixed matrix has not achieved reliable concentration recovery for these simultaneous loadings.

Matrix solvers and concentration-curve checks

We now explicitly remove the measured cross-well contribution before interpreting concentration. Four isolated 10 µM controls define an 8 × 4 matrix from the I and Q responses; the other three wells contain 100 µL DDW in each control. After subtracting the current water blank, a signed ridge solution estimates the four source contributions. Its strength is chosen using only single-source and blank technical holdouts, without fitting the tested mixed or uniform loadings.

Simply adding a scalar curve after this fixed-matrix inversion does not repair its transfer. The inferred D3 source component decreases across part of the uniform series. None of the tested post-matrix linear, quadratic, exponential, power or PCHIP families provides increasing, valid in-range inverses for all four components. Signed matrix estimates are therefore retained as diagnostics. This does not rule out other nonlinear forward models, which these data have not validated.

Optical specifications

Match the spectra

The optical chain needs both excitation and separation: blue light excites GCaMP6f, while the receiver filter passes green fluorescence to a photodiode that responds to both colors. The spectra connect the biological reporter to the filter and detector choices. [14]

Component sources and pin mapping

Circuit components

The power path uses TPS61023 boost conversion [2] and BQ24074 charging [3]. ESP32-WROOM-32E coordinates acquisition [4]. LP5907 and LTC6655 provide regulation and a 2.5 V reference [5] [6]; an LMP7721 TIA and ADS8866 16-bit SAR ADC form the analog readout [7] [8]. Four Würth blue LEDs and a VEMD5060X01 photodiode provide the optical path [9] [10].

Firmware assigns LED gates to GPIO 25/26/27/32 and ADC CONVST/SCLK/DOUT to 33/18/19. Its 4 kHz timebase specifies 8,000 samples per two-second I/Q block at 41/67/89/113 Hz. The firmware configuration retains BOARD_VALIDATED=false.

Mechanical drawings and assembly checks
Technical drawings · exploded assembly, fan and ports

Assembly checkpoints

The assembly specification protects the electronics and keeps the well vessels replaceable.

  1. With power disconnected, inspect all eight prints and measure PCB, mounting holes, component heights and antenna insertion clearance.
  2. Mount the PCB with M2.5 screws, nuts and suitable washers. Mount the fan with M3 fasteners and matched spacers, checking blade clearance; never force a tight part into place with screws.
  3. Install the filter and optical seals, locate the four wells by their flanges, then fit the well seals and removable compression plate.
  4. Connect gas tubes to the gas lid and route them through the side openings; close the lid without displacing or pinching seals.
  5. Perform an off-board cell-free liquid leak check, then verify electrical rails, raw ADC headroom, stop control and actual LED orientation before acquisition.

The CAD includes an antenna aperture with about 0.66 mm nominal insertion clearance against the supplied drawing.

Interface records and firmware details

Signal correction

The correction routine subtracts an independent illuminated blank in complex I/Q space and uses a separate fluorescence phase reference. Exports preserve raw I/Q, unsmoothed corrected values, signed projection, calibration identifiers and quality flags. This path is verified by software tests; a living-cell baseline remains a separate quantity.

Firmware and interface

J2 supplies USB-C power. A 3.3 V USB–UART adapter connects through J3; hold SW3 while resetting with SW2 to enter upload mode. The ESP32 firmware and LittleFS interface form one software installation.
