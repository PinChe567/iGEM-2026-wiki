What do we mean by sustainability?

Sustainability describes how ecological, economic and social systems interact over time. These interconnected systems create "wicked problems": no single correct answer and no single discipline can solve them [3]. Systems thinking helps us follow consequences beyond one component; resilience is the capacity to adapt while retaining core function, and matters alongside efficiency.

The Brundtland definition (1987) asks us to meet present needs without compromising future generations [1]. It is accessible but criticized as anthropocentric. The environmental, social and economic "three pillars" emphasize interdependence. The UN SDGs [2] translate these ideas into 17 goals for 2030. Despite criticism of their breadth, they provide a common language for iGEM, which we use with attention to those limits.

AeroSense and sustainable development

AeroSense is a portable, cartridge-based VOC (volatile organic compound) sensing platform combining living olfactory receptors, custom lock-in optoelectronics and a neuromorphic AI classifier modeled on the fly brain. We distinguish two layers throughout this page:

  • Application theme — stored food (Food & Nutrition track). Mold- and pathogen-VOC screening in stored tea, coffee, and other dried foods. Our public survey and most of our education work belong here.
  • Commercial beachhead — fruit export supply chain. Early detection of fungal disease and ripeness in Taiwan's banana and mango exports, with tea processing as the second-stage expansion. Its buyers are institutions (exporters, packing houses, associations) rather than individual consumers, which is why we entered here first.

We asked not only whether the technology could work, but whether it should, for whom, and at what cost to which other goal. Our Human Practices engagement shaped the answers: the Taoyuan District Agricultural Improvement Station's Plant Protection Lab, the Taiwan Banana Research Institute, farmers' associations and facility farms, an electronic-nose company's Chief Science Officer, Chia-Nien Biotechnology, an analytical-instrumentation (GC-MS/LC-MS) professional, a patent attorney, our academic advisors, and the pupils, students, and peer teams we taught and learned from.

We focus on SDGs 2, 9, 12 and 17, supported by SDGs 3, 4 and 10. Each goal connects our work to an intended benefit, the evidence available and a next measurement. The discussion below brings together life-cycle, cost, market and validation questions.

SDG 2 — Zero Hunger

Commercial beachhead. Earlier disease screening and better-timed ripeness decisions aim to reduce loss along the fruit export supply chain.

Target 2.4 — Resilient agricultural practices. Field interviews informed our estimate of roughly NT$3.51 billion/year in fungal-disease-related risk in Taiwan's banana and mango export sector: about 33.6% of NT$10.445 billion annual output. Visual sampling can detect infection only after symptoms appear, while destructive laboratory assays limit shipment-wide coverage. Our proposed VOC screen would help exporters, packing houses and cooperatives isolate, reroute or re-time a batch earlier. Globally, roughly 14% of food is lost between harvest and retail [4].

Next step: interview an exporter or packing house, obtain batch-level loss records, and compare detection lead time with visual symptom onset. Current interviews cover upstream agricultural institutions; batch-level loss has not been measured.

Target 2.3 — Productivity and incomes of small-scale producers. The instrumentation professional expected low willingness to pay among individual farmers. An association- or cooperative-owned reader could share the cost and protect smallholders' income without requiring each farmer to buy a device. Next step: model who pays, who benefits and whether the benefit reaches smallholders.

SDG 9 — Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure

AeroSense integrates biological sensing, custom optoelectronics and neuromorphic AI, building on peer-reviewed research by our academic advisors.

Target 9.5 — Research and technological capability. The e-nose CSO told us the industry bottleneck is the sensing element — selectivity, sensitivity, lifetime, reproducibility. AeroSense responds with a living, self-renewing receptor (Drosophila OR/Orco with the GCaMP6 reporter in a standard mammalian cell line), structurally different from a static chemical film. Our classifier is modeled on the fly mushroom body's PN–KC–MBON circuit and has a direct published lineage through our advisors' Science Advances paper [5], which is open access (CC BY 4.0), with code on Dryad and data on Zenodo.

Target 9.b — Domestic technology development. Our first readout used a blue LED and a CMOS camera at 30 frames per second. Ambient light and sensor noise motivated a four-channel design: four LEDs modulated at distinct frequencies excite four wells, one photodiode collects the signal, and an ESP32 performs digital lock-in detection. The current Hardware record includes a fabricated PCB and assembled enclosure. Excitation leakage at the signal frequency survives lock-in, and shared-detector and gas-space mixing require emission filtering, blank wells and per-well calibration. Validation proceeds through five stages: component bench tests, electronics-only lock-in, fluorescent-dye calibration, environmental-noise robustness and live-cell end-to-end testing. Next step: measure noise rejection, crosstalk and full-reader power as each stage completes, and include culture media, consumables and cold-chain requirements in the footprint comparison.

SDG 12 — Responsible Consumption and Production

Non-destructive sampling and a reusable cartridge are intended to reduce discarded produce and single-use assay materials. Risk tiers connect a screening signal to a proportionate follow-up decision.

Target 12.3 — Food loss. Our stored-food survey identified a decision gap: 78% understood that removing visible mold does not always make food safe, but only 58% understood that a normal look and smell does not mean no risk; 27% were unsure and 15% held the misconception. Risk-tier communication therefore needs to address both unnecessary discard and unsafe retention, with confirmatory testing for concerning samples. These self-selected, self-reported responses guide design; they do not measure population prevalence or the amount of food discarded. Next step: include shops and warehouses, and test risk-tier communication with users.

Target 12.5 — Waste prevention and reuse. Destructive assays such as PCR, ELISA and GC-MS sample preparation consume material and generate single-use waste. Our cartridge is designed for non-destructive sampling and repeated readings. A living cell population could extend replacement intervals compared with the near-daily replacement described for some conventional e-nose sensors by industry; a lifetime test must establish this. Next step: test cartridge lifetime, compare it with a destructive-assay baseline in a screening LCA, and evaluate recyclable or biodegradable housing.

Target 12.4 — Chemicals and waste over the life cycle. Our wet-lab audit will classify reagents by hazard, quantify plastic waste in kg and other culture consumables, and identify segregation and reuse practices, following self-audits published by previous iGEM teams. Quantities have not yet been measured. Next step: complete the audit before the wiki freeze.

Also connects to

  • SDG 3 — Good Health and Well-being. A detected VOC signal is not a food-safety verdict, so AeroSense is a screening tool that recommends confirmatory testing, never a diagnostic replacement. The risk-tier output is the design response. A possible medical direction, raised in early discussions with the S.Y.Dao Cancer Prevention Foundation, is only exploratory: it would bring regulatory demands that our current agricultural and food screening does not face.
  • SDG 4 — Quality Education. Our education work addresses the survey's specific misconceptions through a free five-module Learning Lab, a science camp for upper-grade pupils at a rural elementary school, a university workshop and a reusable teacher toolkit. Education documents participant feedback and learner-created work; measured learning gains require a separate evaluation.
  • SDG 10 — Reduced Inequalities. The farmer-adoption-resistance finding is also an inequality risk: an individual-farmer product would likely reach only well-resourced buyers. Targeting associations and cooperatives is our current response — not yet a validated equitable outcome.

Limitations and tensions

  • Our own waste (SDG 12). Living-cell cartridges are themselves a consumable. Lower replacement frequency is our hypothesis; only the LCA can confirm a net benefit.
  • Who benefits from innovation (SDG 9 vs. SDG 2/10). Patents are necessary for commercial defensibility, and we are preparing a filing through our university. But restrictive licensing could limit affordability and decide whether exporters or smallholder-serving associations get access.
  • Open science vs. patent timing. Our patent attorney advised that our own early disclosure can later count against novelty, so we are holding back some technical detail until a filing is made. This sits uneasily with iGEM's open-science norms and with our wish to leave reusable work for future teams. We resolve it by sequencing — file first, then publish — and by keeping our education materials fully open, because they do not depend on that detail.
  • Partnership maturity (SDG 17). Presenting every interview as a partnership would overstate how far our validation has gone; hence the maturity table above.
  • Resilience vs. equity (SDG 2). A tool that makes exporters more resilient can still fail smallholders if the economics do not flow downstream. We do not assume the two move together.

Impact and future plan

Social. Smallholders and their cooperatives are the priority beneficiaries. We plan institution-level purchasing and plain-language risk tiers that guide farmers and households while keeping the tool a screening aid. Free, reusable education materials let teachers and other teams run sessions independently.

Economic. Earlier detection targets about NT$3.51 billion/year in sector-level risk. Savings claims require an exporter or packing-house interview and a cost model. As on our Entrepreneurship page, the first market is banana and mango, followed by tea.

Environmental. We aim to reduce discarded food and single-use assay waste. LCA and cartridge-lifetime testing must establish whether the living-cell approach provides a net benefit.

For future teams. The open questions and next steps above provide a starting point for next season's team or another group.

Conclusion

AeroSense connects agricultural resilience, biological sensing, local technology and shared knowledge. Next tests address cartridge waste, patent access, disclosure timing and benefits for smallholders, with each proposed benefit tied to evidence, a metric and an action.

Earlier project framing

01 · Our approach

Benefits, burdens and evidence

AeroSense links a living olfactory-receptor layer, custom fluorescence readout and a fly-inspired classifier. Sustainability asks whether that system improves a real food decision, who benefits, and what resources it consumes. Our Human Practices work guides that question; every potential benefit below is paired with a limitation and a next measurement. [1]

One connected system

The Brundtland framework asks us to meet present needs while preserving future generations’ ability to meet theirs. For AeroSense, that means considering food loss, affordability and the footprint of the device together. [2]

Systems thinking helps us follow consequences beyond the sensor: a useful alert could reduce late discard, while cartridges add waste or pricing excludes smaller producers. Resilience means retaining a useful function as conditions change. [3] We use the UN Sustainable Development Goals to organize these connected questions. [4]

Four goals, four tests

Resilient agriculture and smallholder income

Next: interview an exporter or packing house, measure lead time, and trace who receives the economic benefit.

Biological sensing and local technology

Targets 9.5 / 9.b. Living OR/Orco cells, a team-designed ESP32 reader and fly-inspired classification form our research approach.

Next: complete staged validation and measure power across the reader and recurring biological workflow.

Food decisions, reuse and our own waste

Targets 12.3 / 12.5 / 12.4. Headspace sampling aims to preserve the food sample. Cartridge reuse and clearer risk information remain design intentions.

Next: test cartridge lifetime, audit laboratory consumables, and compare complete workflows in a screening LCA.

Detailed SDG pathways

05 · Pathways

Four impact pathways

Each pathway connects an SDG target, the problem, our proposed mechanism, supporting evidence, risks and the next measurement.

Our source identifies SDGs 2, 9, 12 and 17 as primary goals. SDGs 3, 4 and 10 connect screening boundaries, reusable education and equitable access. The SDGs organize our questions; they do not establish impact.

SDG 2 — Zero Hunger

Track A primary

  1. Target

    Target 2.4 — more resilient agricultural practices along the supply chain. Target 2.3 — smallholder productivity and incomes — is the tension we refuse to hide.

  2. Problem

    The proposed application is earlier disease and ripeness triage in banana and mango supply chains. The commercial plan currently narrows to banana disease screening. Visual inspection and laboratory confirmation enter different points in that workflow; their turnaround and cost must be measured with the intended buyer. Entrepreneurship holds the market assumptions (EL-03).

    • Estimated

  3. Mechanism

    Designed VOC risk tiers that could inform isolate / reroute / time-shipment decisions before symptoms are obvious. This is an aim — not a demonstrated loss reduction.

    • Hypothesis
    • Designed

  4. Evidence

    Stakeholder and gap status: EL-04, EL-05, EL-09, EL-17.

    • Stakeholder-backed

  5. Boundary / risk

    A tool that improves exporter resilience can still fail Target 2.3 if value never reaches smallholders — or if individual farmers resist a device that flags their own product (EL-05).

  6. Next measurement

    Interview at least one exporter or packing house; compare VOC-screening time with visual symptom onset; collect batch-level loss records and model whether shared access protects smallholder income.

SDG 9 — Industry, Innovation & Infrastructure

Shared stack

  1. Target

    Target 9.5 — enhance research and industrial technological capability. Target 9.b — support domestic technology development.

  2. Problem

    Sensing-element performance (selectivity, sensitivity, lifetime, reproducibility) is the hard constraint — not electronics or algorithms alone. Stakeholder record: Human Practices.

    • Stakeholder-backed

  3. Mechanism

    Living Drosophila OR/Orco + GCaMP reporter cells as a swappable sensing element; team-designed ESP32-standalone four-channel FDM/DLIA reader; neuromorphic classification with a published research lineage through advisors. Portable architecture is intended — not field-validated.

    • Designed

  4. Evidence

    The advisors’ published mushroom-body research provides a lineage for the classifier, not a test of AeroSense’s agricultural accuracy. [6] The P2 plate-reader pilot adds preliminary laboratory observations; it does not validate the complete cartridge-to-classifier system. Design and gap status: EL-06, EL-13, EL-14.

    • Designed

  5. Boundary / risk

    Living cells add containment and consumable requirements. Pricing and licensing also affect access. EL-16

  6. Next measurement

    Complete component tests, lock-in readout, fluorescence-standard calibration, environmental-noise checks and live-cell end-to-end testing. Record measured power at each relevant stage and include culture, consumables and cold-chain requirements in the footprint boundary.

SDG 12 — Responsible Consumption & Production

Tracks A + B

  1. Target

    12.3 food loss/waste; 12.5 waste reduction through reuse; 12.4 sound management of chemicals and wastes — including our own.

  2. Problem

    Late spoilage detection can lead to batch rejection. Screening may also create unnecessary discard if false positives are acted on. The relevant comparison includes food decisions, sample preparation, reagents and consumables in each workflow.

  3. Mechanism

    The architecture combines headspace sensing, modular cartridges and risk-tier communication. The footprint inventory includes cartridge replacement.

    • Designed
    • Hypothesis

  4. Evidence

    Survey / design / gap status: EL-01, EL-02, EL-08, EL-10, EL-12.

    • Early survey aggregate

  5. Boundary / risk

    Living-cell cartridges are themselves consumables. “Self-renewing cells ⇒ lower replacement frequency” remains a hypothesis until lifetime and LCA exist (EL-10, EL-12). We do not claim a net environmental benefit.

  6. Next measurement

    Verify the expanded survey and test risk-tier comprehension; measure usable cartridge lifetime; complete a wet-lab waste and hazard log; compare the full screening workflow with a defined confirmation workflow in a screening LCA.

Life-cycle assessment framework

Measuring net benefit

The functional unit is one tonne of a defined food lot screened through a stated storage and decision interval, compared with the same lot under the existing workflow. No avoided-food-loss or carbon result has been measured. This model makes the required measurements explicit instead of assigning a benefit to an SDG label.

Table 3. Definitions, units and required inputs for estimating retained food mass, net emissions and operating value per tonne screened. Sensitivity ranges are mathematical bounds; no current measured net benefit or carbon break-even value is asserted.
QuantityDefinition / equationInput evidence and sensitivity
Food retained ΔM (kg / tonne screened)Baseline discarded mass − screened-workflow discarded massMatched lots and follow-up outcomes needed. Count unnecessary discard from false positives; no current measured value.
Net emissions ΔE (kg CO₂e / tonne)ΔM × food emission factor − added device, cartridge, energy and disposal burdenFood-, location- and system-boundary-specific factor required. No default factor is assigned. A negative result means added emissions.
Net operating value ΔC (NTD / tonne)ΔM × recoverable unit value − testing, confirmation, labor, handling and disposal costsRecoverable value is not retail price by default. No shipment ledger or verified test-cost input exists.
Sensitivity to actionVary avoided-loss fraction and additional-discard fraction over 0–1These are mathematical bounds, not observed rates. Their combination must remain consistent with the lot’s actual loss and rejection fractions.
Carbon break-even (kg retained / tonne)Additional system burden ÷ food emission factor, when factor > 0A decision threshold only; not calculated until both inputs are traceable.
Safety constraintFood retained cannot be counted as a benefit if it introduces an unacceptable safety outcomeConfirmatory workflow and false-negative outcomes remain separate from mass and carbon accounting.

The model cannot turn a VOC alert into evidence that food is safe. A pilot must compare the decisions and outcomes produced by the two workflows, not simply multiply a claimed detection rate by national food losses. Download the input and equation template (CSV).
