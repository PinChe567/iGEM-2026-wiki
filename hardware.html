Hardware

Building a reader around light, airflow and living samples

  1. Home
  2. Dry Lab
  3. Hardware

Abstract

AeroSense is a compact reader designed to compare odor-responsive living-cell samples through fluorescence. It combines four replaceable 100 µL wells under a shared gas chamber, four frequency-tagged blue LEDs, one filtered photodiode, an ESP32-based acquisition circuit and a local phone interface. An eight-part printed enclosure fixes the optical geometry, separates sample gas from electronics cooling and allows the wells to be replaced. Optical simulations supported a single LEE 101 layer, which retained 70.43% of proxy-green current while reducing LED-derived current to 2.987% of the unfiltered value; airflow modeling guided the inlet shelf and delivery conditions. The reader was assembled, and its acquisition software supports frequency-resolved records and local data export. Assembly feedback informed the revised developer manual. Together, these results establish the prototype and its operating workflow; fluorescence detection by the complete instrument remains to be verified.

Introduction

Monitoring volatile changes around stored produce requires a practical link between receptor biology and an interpretable measurement. Our sensing strategy pairs insect odorant receptors with the calcium indicator GCaMP6f so that receptor activation can be read optically. [13] [14] The hardware task is to expose several cell samples to the same gas input and record their optical responses in a compact reader with replaceable wells.

A difference between channels should reflect the samples being compared, rather than differences in gas delivery or light collection. We therefore need to control the gas path, keep each well in a fixed position and limit excitation light reaching the detector. Using one photodiode simplifies the detector layout, but requires distinct LED modulation frequencies and digital lock-in processing to recover the excitation channels. These requirements connect the enclosure, optics, electronics and acquisition software.

Airflow

All four wells share one gas input, so a difference in exposure could be confused with a difference in receptor response. We compared the open lid, perforated plate and integral shelf to ask how each directs the inlet jet and delivers gas toward the liquid surfaces.

The printed gas lid carries an inlet and outlet on opposite sides of one shared chamber. Its integral shelf redirects the incoming stream above the four well openings. A removable compression plate presses the sealing stack around the wells, while flexible couplers connect the gas ports to tubing through the side openings.

Inlet filter→Gas lid and shelf→Shared well headspace→Outlet filter→Controlled suction

In the video, first compare jet direction and speed across the three geometries, then follow the concentration front as the shelf flow rate changes. The arrival-time calculation uses concentration beside each liquid surface, connecting the flow pattern to sample exposure.

At 3 mL/min with 100 µL in each well, the model predicts gas concentration beside the liquid surface reaching 10% of the inlet concentration in 5.30–6.29 s and 90% in 47.80–49.42 s.

At matched conditions (3 mL/min, 100 µL/well and D = 10 mm²/s), the open chamber, 12-hole plate and current shelf had normalized downward-momentum proxies of 1.00, 0.57 and 0.69. Their slowest-well t90 values were 51.67, 48.36 and 49.42 s, with interwell spreads of 4.64, 2.83 and 1.62 s, respectively. We retained the shelf: it reduced the momentum proxy by 31% and brought all four wells to t90 within approximately 50 s, meeting our provisional 60 s gas-transport target. Reusing the completed integrated enclosure also avoided another fabrication cycle. The plate reduced momentum more and remains a candidate for the next version.

Here, t90 is the time for mean gas concentration beside each liquid surface to reach 90% of the inlet value; it excludes external tubing and cellular response. Similar velocities do not guarantee matched concentration arrival, because diffusion also affects transport. These results on the current grid do not establish fully uniform exposure. The fixed-surface boundary did not solve flow in the medium or forces on cells, so it cannot establish cell safety. A coupled fluid–fluid model would need to solve both phases and their interfacial force balance, as described by COMSOL [24].

Optics

Well illumination

Our design uses one photodiode to read four wells, with LED modulation distinguishing the excitation channels. Light can still reach neighboring wells, so a frequency channel may contain fluorescence from more than its intended sample. The optical model therefore asks how well the geometry preserves each well’s contribution.

In the transfer matrix below, compare the diagonal contributions from the intended well with off-diagonal contributions from neighboring wells. Detailed simulations and the optical-path animation are presented in Model →.

The well simulation predicts unequal collection and mixing between wells: the intended well contributes 88.6–90.9% of the modeled green signal in each LED channel. A brighter channel can therefore reflect collection geometry as well as sample fluorescence. We keep the well–LED–detector registration fixed and specify calibration with the same fluorescence standard placed in one well at a time. These four measurements define a measured 4 × 4 transfer matrix for correcting unequal sensitivity and estimating neighboring-well contributions. Its correction must then recover independent mixed-well standards; the simulated matrix is a design guide, not a calibration applied to the device.

Filter selection and spectral alignment

The photodiode responds to both blue excitation and green reporter light. Filter selection therefore balances rejection of excitation leakage against retention of the signal band. The comparison below holds the source and geometry assumptions fixed: read the LED-derived and proxy-green currents separately, then compare the resulting raw peak.

A single LEE 101 layer retains 70.43% of proxy-green current while reducing LED-derived current to 2.987% of the unfiltered model. The filter sits over the collection aperture, leaving the excitation path open.

The filter comparison depends on where excitation, emission and detector sensitivity overlap. In the first panel, compare the LED spectrum with GCaMP6f excitation and locate its green emission. In the second, compare that emission band with filter transmission and photodiode responsivity to follow the collection path.

Electronics

Signal and control

To compare optical channels, we must convert photocurrent into a timed electrical record. The diagram traces the main readout path and distinguishes the LED-control and power branches.

Schematic

Photodiode → TIA → ADC → ESP32

Four independently driven LEDs and one shared photodiode reduce the number of detector channels while preserving a route to frequency-resolved readout. This choice places the burden on stable acquisition and measured optical calibration.

The acquisition specification uses a 4 kHz timebase, two-second I/Q blocks and 41/67/89/113 Hz LED channels.

The acquisition retains in-phase and quadrature components at each excitation frequency, following Scofield’s frequency-domain treatment of lock-in detection. [1]

Keep cooling separate from sample gas

Interface and firmware

One local interface runs measurements, displays channel signals and saves named records. History reopens earlier results; JSON and CSV exports retain the acquisition settings and raw data.

  1. Connect

    Connect to the reader locally and confirm the active data source. The same interface supports device acquisition and a clearly labeled demonstration mode.

  2. Acquire

    Name the sample and choose a single measurement or scheduled acquisition. Advanced settings control block count, interval, LED duty and active LEDs. Each block records two seconds; stopping turns the LEDs off.

  3. Inspect

    Read the sample count, ADC voltage, channel signals and quality flags together. ADC diagnostics compare hardware SPI with slow GPIO sampling and test the data-line response to weak pulls.

  4. Reopen and export

    Open a selected history record, reload JSON or CSV, and export named files. Browser storage retains available raw samples for later inspection.

Cell viability

A living-cell cartridge needs recoverable viable cells as well as suitable optical geometry. We examined cells recovered from the medium and well bottom after the same incubation. Panel A compares viability in these paired fractions; Panel B converts bottom counts to nominal live and dead recovery density. Read the individual well points alongside the mean and spread.

After 7 h in the printed wells, recovered HEK293–GCaMP6f cells showed 60.0 ± 7.5% viability in the medium and 25.5 ± 0.9% in the bottom recovery fraction (mean ± SD, three paired wells).

Wells contained 100 µL of a nominal 2 × 10⁶ cells/mL suspension at 37°C and 5% CO₂. The estimated live bottom recovery was 49,667 ± 4,805 cells per well. Recovery estimates use nominal volume and area; bottom recovery can include settled cells and does not measure attachment.

Board acquisition

For the board check, we inspected sample count, timing flags and variation in raw ADC codes, then compared hardware SPI with slower GPIO sampling. Reading these together distinguishes successful data transfer from a changing input signal.

Two board recordings each acquired 8,000 samples with no reported timing fault. Every ADC code was 32767. The ADC diagnostic captured another 192 readings across hardware SPI, slow GPIO and restored SPI, again all 32767.

Acquisition completed, but the invariant codes did not demonstrate a light-dependent response. The run labeled “Dark” also had all four LEDs enabled, so it cannot serve as an LED-off baseline.

Discussion

The project now has an assembled eight-part reader, a tested acquisition and export workflow, and manuals revised from an assembly trial. Its main contribution is the integration of replaceable living-cell wells, a shared gas chamber and frequency-tagged optical readout in one serviceable enclosure. Fixed optical registration makes repeated calibration possible; separate cooling and sample-gas paths keep fan operation from directly setting sample delivery. The removable wells also let us change the cell-contact material without rebuilding the electronics.

Optical and airflow models turned these design choices into testable targets: the single LEE 101 layer limits excitation leakage, the inlet shelf changes the incoming jet, and the transfer matrix identifies unequal well collection. The next milestone is measured performance: verify ADC response with known electrical inputs, establish fluorescence-standard response and background stability, then test the measured single-well calibration against independent mixed-well samples. Gas-tracer measurements at the wells should separately test the modeled arrival times.

The cell-recovery experiment shows that viable cells can be recovered from the printed wells, but lower viability in the bottom fraction cannot be assigned to the resin, culture conditions or recovery procedure without a matched control. Published tests of other stereolithography resins show that material choice and post-processing can affect cell compatibility [21].

Machined acrylic (PMMA) wells and a poly-D-lysine adhesion treatment are candidates for the next comparison. PMMA has supported HEK293 electrophysiology in another device [22], and poly-D-lysine improved HEK293 attachment in a separate culture assay [23]. We would compare untreated and treated wells with standard culture plastic under matched seeding, medium, incubation and recovery conditions, measuring viability, retained-cell number, optical background and reporter response before selecting a material or coating.

The fixed ADC codes observed on the board prevent the successful software checks from establishing optical detection. Resolving that readout fault and improving cell-contact conditions are therefore the immediate priorities. A subsequent independent assembly trial should record completion, assistance, elapsed time and alignment errors to test whether the revised manual improves the complete operating workflow.

Conclusion

AeroSense combines four removable wells, filtered optical collection, shared-photodiode electronics and a local phone interface in a documented build. Simulations support the chosen light and gas paths. Cell-recovery and board tests establish current limits in culture performance and analog readout.

Component sources and pin mapping

Circuit components

The power path uses TPS61023 boost conversion [2] and BQ24074 charging [3]. ESP32-WROOM-32E coordinates acquisition [4]. LP5907 and LTC6655 provide regulation and a 2.5 V reference [5] [6]; an LMP7721 TIA and ADS8866 16-bit SAR ADC form the analog readout [7] [8]. Four Würth blue LEDs and a VEMD5060X01 photodiode provide the optical path [9] [10].

Firmware assigns LED gates to GPIO 25/26/27/32 and ADC CONVST/SCLK/DOUT to 33/18/19. Its 4 kHz timebase specifies 8,000 samples per two-second I/Q block at 41/67/89/113 Hz. The firmware configuration retains BOARD_VALIDATED=false.

Assembly checkpoints

The assembly specification protects the electronics and keeps the well vessels replaceable.

  1. With power disconnected, inspect all eight prints and measure PCB, mounting holes, component heights and antenna insertion clearance.
  2. Mount the PCB with M2.5 screws, nuts and suitable washers. Mount the fan with M3 fasteners and matched spacers, checking blade clearance; never force a tight part into place with screws.
  3. Install the filter and optical seals, locate the four wells by their flanges, then fit the well seals and removable compression plate.
  4. Connect gas tubes to the gas lid and route them through the side openings; close the lid without displacing or pinching seals.
  5. Perform an off-board cell-free liquid leak check, then verify electrical rails, raw ADC headroom, stop control and actual LED orientation before acquisition.

The CAD includes an antenna aperture with about 0.66 mm nominal insertion clearance against the supplied drawing.

Interface records and firmware details

Signal correction

The correction routine subtracts an independent illuminated blank in complex I/Q space and uses a separate fluorescence phase reference. Exports preserve raw I/Q, unsmoothed corrected values, signed projection, calibration identifiers and quality flags. This path is verified by software tests; a living-cell baseline remains a separate quantity.

Firmware and interface

J2 supplies USB-C power. A 3.3 V USB–UART adapter connects through J3; hold SW3 while resetting with SW2 to enter upload mode. The ESP32 firmware and LittleFS interface form one software installation.

Standard-sequence specification

After unpowered fit and leak checks, confirm electrical rails, ADC headroom, DARK acquisition, stop control and observed D1–D4 mapping. Then use a traceable sodium-fluorescein standard: illuminated blank B_CAL → independent B_CHECK → four single-well C conditions → all wells at 0.5C → all wells at C → repeat 0.5C. These are nine illuminated analytical conditions, each with at least five distinct valid two-second technical blocks; DARK is saved separately.

The protocol specifies a 50/100 nM pilot and records fill, medium/pH, LED duty and filter. Its approximately five-second record spacing can miss fast GCaMP6f peaks; dye measurements and cell assays address different signals.

Optical output

Concentration response, a 4 × 4 single-well matrix and held-out blank before/after correction. The analysis rejects DEMO, duplicate or incompatible records.

Independent assembly

The manual-evaluation specification covers 2–3 non-designers and records completion, time, assistance, orientation and seal errors.
