Engineered OR/Orco cells are designed to generate calcium-dependent GCaMP fluorescence after receptor activation. Biological rationale: Design. Hardware is a Dry Lab track and the READ layer of the Sense → Read → Decode → Act architecture (not a peer workstream of Wet Lab): it converts that weak biological fluorescence into a quantitative digital signal for later computational interpretation.

The detector must recover small fluorescence changes while facing excitation-light leakage, ambient illumination, electronic noise, high-impedance PCB leakage, drift, and optical crosstalk. Measurement integrity, rather than nominal ADC resolution alone, is the main design objective.

Engineering calculations

First-order design tools from the hardware design report. They are not assembled-reader measurements. Where the design study and the current schematic differ, both numbers are shown.

LED series resistor

R = (VSUPPLY − VF) / I

5 V rail, 20 mA datasheet optical point, Würth 155124BS73200:

  • VF max 3.8 V → R ≈ (5 − 3.8) / 0.020 = ~60 Ω
  • VF typical 3.4 V → R ≈ (5 − 3.4) / 0.020 = ~80 Ω

100 Ω was selected as a conservative prototype start: ~16 mA at typical VF, ~12 mA at max VF, both below the 20 mA optical specification. Electrical power at the typical point is ~54 mW, not emitted optical power.

Photodiode current

IPD ≈ POPT × Rλ

7.5 mm² VEMD5060X01. POPT at the detector is not a datasheet freeze and has not been measured on the assembled reader, so no photocurrent is claimed here.

Why hardware?

A living OR/Orco cell reports receptor activation as GCaMP fluorescence. Biological rationale: Design. Hardware exists to turn that weak optical change into a documented digital measurement.

The problem is not simply detecting light. Shared microscopes and plate readers can see fluorescence, but they do not automatically give AeroSense a portable, versioned, multi-channel reader whose excitation, gain, and demodulation are part of the measurement system. A dedicated reader is required because the biological signal is small and the optical/electronic environment is hostile.

Primary takeaway. The problem is not simply detecting light. The problem is preserving measurement integrity.

We translated the biological readout into six engineering requirements. We intentionally separate specification, calculation, and experimental validation. Component specifications establish a reasonable starting design; only measurements on the assembled reader can establish its actual noise floor, sensitivity, crosstalk, and detection limit.

Requirement Engineering response
Detect weak fluorescence Large-area photodiode + high-gain, ultra-low-bias-current TIA
Excite GCaMP reproducibly Four approximately 470 nm side-view LEDs with controlled switching
Distinguish optical channels Frequency-division multiplexing (FDM)
Reject non-synchronous interference Digital lock-in amplification (DLIA)
Prevent the electronics from becoming the signal Guarded high-impedance PCB layout, short signal paths, controlled grounding and decoupling
Make the system portable and reproducible ESP32-based standalone architecture, rechargeable-power design, open schematic/BOM/firmware/mechanical files

The measurement challenge

A 16-bit converter can distinguish many codes. That is not the same as recovering a small fluorescence change. The reader has to keep the biological signal distinguishable from several documented threats:

  1. 01

    Excitation-light leakage. Blue excitation can reach the photodiode directly. The detector is sensitive far beyond the GCaMP emission band.

  2. 02

    Ambient illumination. Room light and enclosure leaks add unsynchronized photocurrent.

  3. 03

    Electronic noise. Amplifier, reference, and ADC noise all sit on top of the photocurrent.

  4. 04

    High-impedance PCB leakage. At 100 MΩ feedback, board contamination and layout can become comparable to the electrical signal.

  5. 05

    Drift. Offset and baseline movement can masquerade as a slow biological change.

  6. 06

    Optical crosstalk. Four LED channels share one photodiode; channel identity is not automatic.

Primary message. Measurement integrity matters more than nominal ADC resolution.

Engineering the reader

The subsections below record design decisions for the current reader. Calculations are first-order design tools. They are not measured performance.

4.1 Optical design

Status Completed design · enclosure and filter not finalized

We selected the Würth Elektronik 155124BS73200 side-view LED because its dominant wavelength is specified at approximately 470 nm. At 20 mA, the manufacturer reports a typical forward voltage of 3.4 V, a maximum of 3.8 V, and a 120° viewing angle Würth Elektronik · 155124BS73200 datasheet ↗ (opens in a new tab).

The side-view package allows excitation light to enter the sensing region laterally rather than directly from above. Our optical concept positions the excitation LEDs around the sensing region while the photodiode collects fluorescence along a different optical direction. This approximately orthogonal geometry is intended to reduce direct excitation light reaching the detector.

Geometry alone is not sufficient. The LED has a broad viewing angle, and the photodiode is sensitive to wavelengths from approximately 350 to 1070 nm Vishay · VEMD5060X01 datasheet. The detector therefore responds not only to fluorescence but also to leaked blue excitation light.

For this reason, the final reader requires:

  • an emission-filter strategy;
  • an opaque enclosure;
  • internal optical baffling;
  • fixed LED–sample–detector geometry; and
  • a blank-sample leakage test.

These features are treated as measurement requirements, not cosmetic enclosure features.

The emission-filter model, transmission spectrum, and mechanical geometry will be stated after the optical enclosure is finalized. No filter part number is claimed here.

We cannot calculate the final optical intensity from the LED datasheet alone. The datasheet reports luminous intensity in millicandela under specified electrical conditions Würth Elektronik · 155124BS73200 datasheet ↗ (opens in a new tab). That is useful for component comparison but is not equivalent to the excitation irradiance experienced by the cells. Actual irradiance depends on LED current, viewing angle, distance, enclosure geometry, filter losses, reflections, and optical alignment. Luminous intensity is also photometrically weighted for human vision rather than being a direct measurement of optical power at the excitation wavelength.

Therefore, we do not infer the final fluorescence excitation intensity from the mcd specification alone.

Irradiance or relative optical power at the sample position will be reported after the enclosure is completed, with LED current, duty cycle, distance, measurement instrument, mean ± SD, and between-channel variation.

The LED rail is designed around 5 V because the selected LED can require up to 3.8 V forward voltage under its specified operating condition Würth Elektronik · 155124BS73200 datasheet ↗ (opens in a new tab). A 3.3 V rail would therefore provide insufficient voltage headroom for stable current control near the datasheet operating point. For the first prototype, we selected a 100 Ω series resistor for each LED. That starting point is conservative relative to the 20 mA datasheet optical specification. The LEDs are intended to be modulated rather than operated continuously, reducing average optical exposure and allowing synchronous detection. Final current, duty cycle, sample heating, and photobleaching will be selected from measurements rather than from this calculation alone.

4.2 Photodiode

The VEMD5060X01 is a surface-mount silicon PIN photodiode with a 7.5 mm² sensitive area, package dimensions of approximately 5 × 4 × 0.9 mm, and a spectral sensitivity range of roughly 350–1070 nm Vishay · VEMD5060X01 datasheet.

The large detector area helps light collection, but it also increases detector capacitance compared with smaller photodiodes. Because the photodiode responds strongly outside the target fluorescence band, optical filtering is essential. We therefore chose the detector as a compromise between light collection area ↔ capacitance ↔ spectral selectivity ↔ mechanical size.

4.3 Transimpedance amplifier

The photodiode produces current rather than a directly useful voltage. We therefore use a transimpedance amplifier (TIA). At RF = 100 MΩ, even very small unwanted currents matter. An amplifier input bias current of 1 pA would ideally contribute 100 μV of error.

The LMP7721 is specifically designed for ultra-low input current. Texas Instruments specifies approximately 3 fA typical input bias current, ±20 fA maximum at 25°C, and ±900 fA maximum at 85°C, together with a 17 MHz gain-bandwidth product Texas Instruments · LMP7721 datasheet ↗ (opens in a new tab). That makes the device suitable for a high-impedance photodiode front end—but it does not eliminate PCB leakage. At these current levels, board contamination and layout can become comparable to the electrical signal.

4.5 Feedback compensation

The current schematic uses approximately RF = 100 MΩ and CF = 2.2 pF. A simple parallel-RFCF pole estimate gives ≈723 Hz. That calculation provides an intuitive estimate of feedback-network bandwidth but is not a complete TIA stability analysis. Actual stability also depends on photodiode capacitance, op-amp input capacitance, parasitic PCB capacitance and the LMP7721 frequency response.

Earlier calculations considered 4.7 pF as a more strongly bandwidth-limited configuration. The final capacitor value will therefore be decided using measured settling, noise and frequency response rather than the RC estimate alone.

4.6 Digitization and precision reference

We selected the ADS8866 because it is a 16-bit, 100-kSPS, single-ended SAR ADC supporting a 2.5–5 V external reference and an SPI-compatible digital interface Texas Instruments · ADS8866 datasheet. It is paired with an LTC6655 2.5 V precision reference. The selected LTC6655 B-grade device provides ±0.025% initial accuracy and 2 ppm/°C maximum temperature coefficient Analog Devices · LTC6655 datasheet.

The high sample-rate capability is important not because our biological signal itself changes at 100 kHz, but because the reader uses modulated excitation and digital demodulation.

ADS8866 symbol/pinout correction, 3-wire CS mode, edge damping, and package checks are documented once in §4.8 Symbol, footprint, and package verification Texas Instruments · ADS8866 datasheet. The LTC6655 shutdown pin retains an external 10 kΩ pull-up for normal operation.

A 100 Ω + 1 nF input network is currently used between the TIA output and ADC input to isolate the analog source from SAR sampling transients. This network is not intended to define the biological signal bandwidth; its main purpose is to improve the electrical interface between the TIA and SAR ADC.

4.7 High-impedance PCB design

At a 100 MΩ feedback resistance, PCB layout becomes part of the measurement system. On the current board, the photodiode, LMP7721 input, and feedback network were therefore placed as close together as practical to minimize the high-impedance path. The sensitive photodiode/feedback region is surrounded by a guard structure, while the LMP7721 inverting-input trace is kept deliberately short. Routing from the feedback network toward the amplifier output exits the guarded region through a limited opening rather than crossing the sensitive node unnecessarily Texas Instruments · LMP7721 datasheet ↗ (opens in a new tab).

Component placement was also checked against the optical path: the analog front end should remain close to the photodiode without obstructing the side-view LED–sample–detector geometry.

The current design also separates the 3.3 V analog and digital supply requirements. The PD/TIA/ADC analog front end retains the low-noise LP5907-3.3 regulator for the 3V3_ANA rail, while the higher-current digital rail can use a separate 1 A regulator. This decision prevents the current demand of the ESP32/digital subsystem from forcing us to compromise the low-noise analog supply.

In progress Placement rules are summarized in PCB layout requirements. The analog layout is recorded as a design result dated 11 September; manufacturing checks before fabrication are still open. This page does not treat those two facts as a single freeze.

Multiplexing the signal

One photodiode receives a composite optical signal. To preserve channel identity without replicating the complete analog front end four times, each excitation LED is assigned a different modulation frequency (frequency-division multiplexing, FDM).

The prototype currently uses these provisional excitation frequencies:

Channel 1137 Hz
Channel 2173 Hz
Channel 3211 Hz
Channel 4257 Hz

These values are engineering parameters and will not be presented as final until channel separation and crosstalk have been measured experimentally. The current prototype firmware and project architecture explicitly use four physical channels and these provisional frequencies.

For each target frequency fk, digital lock-in amplification (DLIA) multiplies the digitized detector signal x(t) by synchronized sine and cosine references. Signal components synchronized with a known reference are retained preferentially, while unrelated components are attenuated after integration or low-pass filtering [3] Scofield, 1994 — full entry in References. .

For AeroSense, this architecture is intended to provide two benefits:

  1. channel identification through frequency tagging; and
  2. noise rejection through synchronous detection.

These advantages remain hypotheses until measured on the assembled system. The existing firmware is therefore considered a prototype implementation, not the final validated signal-processing specification.

Next validation steps

Remaining measurement work, stated once. None of the items below is a completed result.

  1. Fabricate and assemble the current board; bring-up power rails and the ESP32.
  2. Measure LED current, channel-to-channel variation, and sample-position irradiance after the enclosure exists.
  3. Measure TIA settling, noise, and stability for the installed RF/CF (do not treat the 4.7 pF study or the 2.2 pF schematic estimate as the answer).
  4. Run the validation ladder on an assembled reader: dark noise, rails, LED modulation, TIA, ADC, linearity, FDM recovery, crosstalk, DLIA SNR, then biological fluorescence.
  5. Publish a freeze-ready BOM CSV only after scripts/check_hardware_bom.py passes.
  6. Release Gerbers, firmware, and a calibration / bring-up document with the freeze package — not draft files labeled as final.

Limitations and next build

At the present stage, several important limitations remain. They are listed here in the open, not inside a collapsed widget.

  1. The final fluorescence photocurrent has not yet been measured, so the optimal TIA gain is not known experimentally.
  2. No experimentally validated detection limit should yet be assigned to the reader.
  3. FDM and DLIA have been implemented conceptually and in prototype firmware but have not yet been characterized on the complete optical-electronic system.
  4. The emission filter, enclosure, rechargeable power subsystem, UI and user workflow remain under development.

These limitations define our next experiments rather than weakening the design process: every remaining claim has a corresponding validation test. These are validation targets, not completed claims.

Optical and electrical hazards for the proposed reader are inventoried on Safety & Security.
