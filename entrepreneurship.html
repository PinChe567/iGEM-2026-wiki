Judging criteria: the business case

Five questions connect the customer need, technical evidence and plan for adoption. Each answer is supported in the sections below.

Who needs it first?

Banana exporters and packing houses need fast on-site screening to decide which samples need laboratory confirmation. Interviews point to research institutes, farmers’ associations and cooperatives as practical pilot channels and potential payers. The product is designed for that inspection decision.

Customer and industry evidence →

What makes the solution feasible?

Four fluorescence channels, with 12/12 single-well identifications were recovered in held-out technical reads of sodium-fluorescein standards. The 10 µM standard was recovered as 9.25–10.83 µM. The design combines interchangeable sensing wells, ESP32 lock-in readout and a fly-inspired classifier. The next milestone is a live-cell odor test that connects these components.

How will it become a product?

Validate → Pilot → Scale is the development route: complete integrated testing, establish repeat use and willingness to pay, then expand across sites and crops. The planning model provides 9–12 months of runway from NT$3.0 million and identifies a three-year financing gap of about NT$4.3 million.

Who can deliver it?

Four founders combine biological sensing, computation and hardware with academic guidance from Prof. Chung-Chuan Lo and Prof. Ching-Che Charng. Agricultural interviews, industry review and startup mentoring shape the plan. Regulatory review, manufacturing and commercial licensing are the next capabilities to secure.

Earlier screening should support a decision, not replace an inspector and could reduce avoidable produce loss. We plan risk tiers with confirmation guidance, explicit partner consent for data sharing, and institutional oversight of cell handling and disposal. Pilot evaluations will measure both benefit and operational burden.

Benefits, risks and responsible use →

Background

Plant disease and post-harvest spoilage cost the food system enormously. The FAO and the International Plant Protection Convention estimate that pests destroy up to 40% of global crops each year, at a cost of about US$220 billion . Even after harvest, roughly 13–14% of food produced is lost between harvest and retail . Bananas show the problem clearly: Fusarium wilt (Panama disease) is a recurring threat to global banana production , and in Taiwan we estimate that fungal-disease-related loss reaches about NT$3.51 billion a year, around 33.6% of the sector's NT$10.445 billion annual output value (team estimate). [2] [3]

The tools used to catch these problems today fall into two groups. Visual inspection is fast but sees only symptoms, by which time disease has often spread or the batch has been mixed with healthy stock. Laboratory assays such as PCR and GC-MS are accurate but slow and impractical to run across a whole shipment, and, as an analytical-instrumentation professional told us, the real barrier is not instrument price but the trained personnel and lab infrastructure they require. Electronic noses could fill the gap, yet the chief science officer of an established domestic electronic-nose company told us that almost no commercial electronic nose has genuinely high selectivity, and that the sensing element itself, not the electronics or algorithms, is the bottleneck.

AeroSense Bio addresses this sensing challenge with a proposed living layer: Drosophila olfactory receptors expressed in mammalian cells, optical calcium-signal readout and a fly-brain-inspired classifier. Replacing the receptor cartridge and its model could let the same reader address different targets.

We considered several applications, including mold detection in stored food, pathogen VOCs and origin verification. Mentor reviews and an industry interview pushed us to start from a single economically quantifiable use. We therefore chose early disease detection in Taiwan's banana export chain as our beachhead , with coffee and tea as later markets. Throughout, we describe the tool as rapid risk screening that recommends laboratory confirmation, because a VOC signal is not the same claim as a safety verdict.

WHO identifies mycotoxins as toxins produced by certain moulds and notes that many survive food processing. This is why our proposed VOC screen must be validated against the relevant reference assay before it can inform a food-safety decision. [8]

About AeroSense Bio

AeroSense Bio is a proposed venture arising from the 2026 NTHU iGEM team. It aims to provide cartridge-based agricultural VOC sensing: a compact reader, replaceable live-cell cartridges and software designed to return a risk tier with a confidence signal. The proposed B2B offering combines device sales, cartridge subscriptions, software and data subscriptions, and custom project services.

We chose B2B because our interviews pointed to institutions, not individual farmers, as the realistic buyers. The instrumentation professional said individual farmers may have low willingness to pay and may resist having their own produce flagged, and the Taiwan Banana Research Institute indicated that an institute like his is more willing to pay and could be an entry point to growers who cannot buy a tool themselves. Selling to a few exporters, packing houses, associations and institutes also costs far less to market than reaching many small farms.

Market Analysis

Customer and Industry Input

Public Interest.

Our public survey on food preservation and mold risk (n=300, bilingual online questionnaire, self-selected respondents, directional only) shows that people understand the risks they can see better than the ones they cannot. 78% knew that removing visible mold does not always make food safe, but only 58% knew that a normal look and smell does not mean there is no risk. Respondents also wanted more than a number. They ranked trustworthy results (76%), accuracy including avoiding false negatives (73%) and clear next-step guidance (68%) ahead of ease of use (61%), cost per test (54%) and speed (49%). This is why AeroSense reports a risk tier with a recommended next step rather than a safe/unsafe verdict. These are consumer responses, peripheral to our agricultural B2B beachhead, and they are not evidence of purchase intent.

Industry Interest.

Taiwan's banana export sector carries an estimated NT$3.51 billion per year in fungal-disease-related loss, about 33.6% of its NT$10.445 billion annual output value (team estimate). We completed the following interviews:

  • Taoyuan District Agricultural Improvement Station, Plant Protection Lab, with farmers' associations and facility farms. There is currently no monitoring beyond looking at the crop, so no data are collected. The researcher also noted that "detects a smell" and "works in a field" are different claims.
  • Pest-and-disease specialist, Taiwan Banana Research Institute. The strongest odors come from belowground and basal tissue, such as corm and pseudostem weevils, soft rot and Fusarium wilt. A faint early signal exists, but a heavy smell can simply reflect a dirty orchard. He said an institute like his is more willing to pay than individual farmers and could be a pilot entry point.
  • Chief Science Officer of an established domestic electronic-nose company. Selectivity, sensitivity, reliability and lifetime, and reproducibility decide whether a sensor is useful. Almost no commercial electronic nose today has genuinely high selectivity, and "the hardware and the algorithms are the easy part."
  • Analytical-instrumentation professional. The barrier to on-site testing is trained personnel and laboratory infrastructure, not instrument price. This led us to position AeroSense as a fast, on-site screening layer with low operator dependence.
  • Challenge Bio (food-safety biotechnology professionals). They advised us to start from the technology, and warned that a mold VOC signal does not necessarily mean food is unsafe. We therefore describe the tool as rapid risk screening, not a safety verdict.

Design Thinking

Minimum Viable Product (MVP)

Current Solutions: Traditional Post-Harvest Inspection

Post-harvest quality control today relies on two disconnected approaches. Visual and manual sampling is fast but catches disease mainly once it is visible, and it depends on individual inspector experience. A researcher at the Taoyuan improvement station told us that there is currently no monitoring method beyond looking at the crop. Laboratory assays are accurate but destructive, and they need trained personnel and laboratory infrastructure, which makes them hard to apply across a full shipment. What is missing is a fast, non-destructive, on-site screening step that tells staff which samples deserve a laboratory test. That is the gap AeroSense aims to fill.

Our Device

AeroSense is a portable, cartridge-based reader designed to expose engineered live-cell wells to sample headspace and read their fluorescence. The proposed product would return a disease-risk tier within a target of minutes. Ripeness forecasting and quality grading are prospective extensions.

User Flow

An operator loads a headspace gas sample into the inlet of a four-well cartridge matched to the target crop and disease, then starts a run from a phone interface. An ESP32 microcontroller drives four blue LEDs (about 468 nm) at distinct modulation frequencies (41, 67, 89 and 113 Hz), exciting HEK293 cells that express an olfactory receptor (OR/Orco) and a GCaMP6 calcium reporter. One shared photodiode, a transimpedance amplifier and a 16-bit ADC capture the fluorescence, and a digital lock-in separates the four channels from background noise. A recognition layer inspired by the fly mushroom body's PN–KC–MBON circuit then classifies the pattern. So far, that layer has been tested only on public metal-oxide sensor datasets, not on AeroSense cell signals.

Core Value

The aim is a decision-ready read-out rather than a raw signal: a risk tier and a recommended next step, such as setting a batch aside for laboratory confirmation. It is a screening layer, not a laboratory replacement and not a safety verdict. Because the cartridge is swappable, the same base device could in principle be redeployed to other targets by developing new cartridges and models. Banana is the first target, and other crops remain hypotheses.

Deliverables and Metrics

The intended output is a risk tier with a recommended action, reflecting the survey’s 73% priority on accuracy and avoiding false negatives. Development metrics include time-to-result, channel signal quality, and sensitivity and specificity against confirmed field outcomes. The provisional gas-transport target is 60 s to all wells; simulation gives a slowest-well t90 of 49.4 s at 3 mL/min. This is gas arrival beside the liquid surface, not total assay time.

Data and Safety

The cartridges use HEK293, a standard mammalian cell line, with insect olfactory receptors expressed in it, so no live insects are involved. The cells are held in sealed wells, and the biosafety requirements for partner sites will be confirmed with institutional guidance before any field deployment.

Scope and Limits
  • Done: the hardware and firmware are designed, the first board is built, and 96 of 96 software checks pass.
  • Measured reader evidence: the expanded study spans 0–20 µM sodium-fluorescein standards and four single-well loading conditions. All 12 held-out technical reads identified the filled well; a 10 µM standard was recovered as 9.25–10.83 µM. Hardware reports the channel-specific curves, cross-response matrix and calibration-transfer comparison.
  • Next instrument steps: test calibration across independent reloadings, validate simultaneous multi-well recovery, and integrate the live-cell workflow. Assembly feedback and the revised manual are documented below.
Who It Serves

Exporters, packing houses, cooperatives and research institutes that need a fast, non-destructive screening step between harvest and shipment. We start with Taiwan's banana export chain. Mango follows, with data collection planned, and high-value tea processing comes in a second phase.

Success Criteria

(1) The staged hardware validation passes its defined acceptance criteria, starting with fluorescence detection by the full instrument.

(2) We complete field trials with agricultural institutions; so far we have completed interviews with the Taoyuan improvement station, farmers' associations, facility farms and the Taiwan Banana Research Institute.

(3) A measured end-to-end time-to-result meets our minutes-scale target on live samples.

(4) Early-adopter interest converts into paid pilots; we have no paying customer yet.

Exit Strategy

At this early stage, our long-term ambition is to grow AeroSense from a single agricultural beachhead into a broader VOC Intelligence Platform spanning food safety, cold chain, and industrial and environmental monitoring. Once the platform is validated across multiple crops, we would pursue either continued growth toward a future financing round or a strategic partnership or acquisition with an established sensing or agri-tech company.

Long-term Impacts

Positive impacts. AeroSense aims to reduce post-harvest agricultural loss and the economic and environmental cost of discarded produce. By pricing on a usage basis rather than a large capital outlay, the platform aims to give smaller exporters and cooperatives — not only large, well-resourced agribusinesses — access to a screening capability that today is effectively limited to buyers who can afford lab-grade testing. Over time, a growing multi-crop VOC dataset could help standardize quality grading across a currently inconsistent, judgment-based system, benefiting both producers (fairer, more transparent grading) and consumers (more reliable food-safety assurance).

Risks to manage. As with any automated risk-classification system, an over-trusted "risk score" could lead a partner to under-inspect a shipment the model misses (a false negative). This is precisely why the team designed the output as a risk tier with escalation guidance rather than a binary pass/fail — a decision informed directly by our public survey finding that false negatives are the least-tolerated failure mode among respondents. Broader deployment of an AI-driven agricultural sensing platform also carries a data-governance responsibility for the VOC and lot-level data collected from partner sites. Our current plan is to keep data project-scoped by default and to share only aggregated, anonymized data for model improvement with explicit partner opt-in — stated here as a policy plan , not yet a built and independently audited system.

As the platform moves from pilot to multi-partner deployment, the team plans to establish a lightweight, mentor-supported data-governance check-in, extending the same "closed feedback loop" discipline it has already applied to its own commercialization process (see Skills, Capabilities & Stakeholders ) to how it manages responsible use of an increasingly large agricultural dataset.

Social Responsibility & SDGs

  • SDG 2 – Zero Hunger. By helping exporters and cooperatives catch disease-affected produce earlier and better time ripeness and quality decisions, AeroSense supports reducing food loss along the agricultural supply chain.
  • SDG 9 – Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure. AeroSense combines biological sensing, custom optoelectronics and neuromorphic computation in a prototype intended for agricultural use. Its classifier draws on peer-reviewed work co-authored by our advisors.
  • SDG 12 – Responsible Consumption and Production. The proposed non-destructive screening workflow aims to avoid unnecessary produce disposal and sampling waste. Its net benefit must include cartridge consumables, cell culture, cleaning and disposal, as assessed on the Sustainability page.
  • SDG 17 – Partnerships for the Goals. Built through active collaboration with an agricultural improvement station, farmers' associations, and NTHU's academic ecosystem, with further partnerships (e.g., with a domestic electronic-nose company) currently in progress.
