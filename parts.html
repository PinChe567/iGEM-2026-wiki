AeroSense Parts Collection

A modular Drosophila olfactory toolkit for building nine receptor-specific living-cell odor sensors with one shared fluorescence readout.

These parts form the biological sensing layer of AeroSense Bio, our platform for early odor-based fungal-risk screening. This wiki page explains and indexes the collection. Authoritative sequence and design records for registered parts live on the iGEM Registry.

One collection, three layers

AeroSense needs two genetic modules co-expressed in the same cell: a sensing module that provides ligand specificity, and a reporting module that converts channel opening into fluorescence. Insect odorant receptors are not GPCRs. The OR–Orco complex is a heteromeric ligand-gated ion channel [1] Sato et al., 2008 — full entry in References & Credits. , so no additional signal-transduction machinery is required in a mammalian host. That is what makes the two-module design sufficient.

Each part occupies exactly one layer, which is what makes the panel modular: the specificity layer can be swapped without touching anything else. Because Orco is universal across insect ORs, the reporting module never changes. Only the sensing module differs between cell lines. Each line contributes one dimension to a response vector; the pattern across all nine — not any single receptor — identifies the odour.

Build an AeroSense sensor

Ligand binding at the OR–Orco channel admits Ca²⁺ [1] Sato et al., 2008 — full entry in References & Credits. . N-terminal GCaMP6f is the designed reporter of that local rise as fluorescence. The sensing cassette is bicistronic; the reporting cassette is one fusion protein. There is no signal peptide in the documented final architecture. Choose a tuning receptor to see that only the OR CDS changes; the reporting module stays put.

Why N-terminal?

Insect ORs and Orco adopt inverted membrane topology relative to vertebrate GPCRs — N-terminus intracellular, C-terminus extracellular [3] Benton et al., 2006 — full entry in References & Credits. , confirmed structurally by cryo-EM of Orco [4] Butterwick et al., 2018 — full entry in References & Credits. . A C-terminal calcium sensor would sit in millimolar extracellular Ca²⁺. N-terminal GCaMP6f stays cytosolic and at the channel mouth.

Design decisions that matter

Why GCaMP6f is N-terminal to Orco

Insect ORs and Orco adopt inverted membrane topology relative to vertebrate GPCRs: N-terminus intracellular, C-terminus extracellular [3] Benton et al., 2006 — full entry in References & Credits. , confirmed structurally for Orco [4] Butterwick et al., 2018 — full entry in References & Credits. . A C-terminal calcium sensor would sit in millimolar extracellular Ca²⁺ and could not report channel opening. N-terminal GCaMP6f stays cytosolic at the channel mouth.

Codon-optimised for human expression

All ten receptor coding sequences and DmOrco were codon-optimised for human expression while preserving the encoded protein. Native Drosophila codon usage is a documented cause of poor OR expression in mammalian cells; optimisation can rescue stable expression in HEK293 [2] Roberts et al., 2021 — full entry in References & Credits. . Each optimised CDS was translated and compared against its FlyBase reference: the proteins are identical to the reference sequences.

Reuse before duplication

We searched the Registry before creating new parts. EMCV IRES and mCherry already existed as byte-identical entries, so we reused BBa_K5490030 and BBa_K4177005 rather than submitting duplicates. A similarly named part, BBa_25V0ZX3W (“mCherry NLS”), is sequence-identical to plain mCherry and was not used.

Keeping first-generation work traceable

BBa_26ACVV3R documents the first-generation Or67b CDS. It is not one of the nine final-panel receptors and it is not a tenth sensing composite. The part is registered so the receptor we began with remains reusable. Its pTwist construct image is filed under first-generation design, not among the ten final composites.

Before you reuse these parts

Four points recorded on our Registry part pages because we could not find them documented elsewhere. They describe limitations, not a claim that the collection is finished.

Published HEK293 evidence is not the same as a VOC-responsive panel

Published HEK293 evidence exists for four receptors in our final panel, but only two belong to the previously reported VOC-responsive panel. Zboray et al. [5] Zboray et al., 2023 — full entry in References & Credits. reported seven VOC-responsive Drosophila OR cell lines; among our nine final receptors, Or85b and Or98a overlap that responsive panel. The same study also generated Or7a and Or19a HEK293 lines that expressed the reporter and responded to the Orco agonist VUAA1, but did not show VOC-specific responses to the tested ligand panel. Each part page states whether published EC₅₀ data exist for that receptor. This is literature context, not AeroSense assay data.

Expression or channel function does not prove VOC-specific detection

Zboray et al. reported OR lines that expressed well and responded to VUAA1 yet gave no VOC-specific response — including lines for Or7a and Or19a, two receptors in our panel [5] Zboray et al., 2023 — full entry in References & Credits. . Receptor-level functional validation is a separate milestone. Expression of the cassette, or a response to an Orco agonist, is not evidence that a given VOC is detected.

mCherry reports transcription, not membrane-localized OR function

In the bicistronic sensing modules, mCherry is translated from the same transcript but as a separate protein. Red fluorescence, if observed, reports transcription and IRES activity. It does not report receptor folding or trafficking to the plasma membrane, and it is not the AeroSense measurement. These constructs carry no epitope tag, so a calcium response — if obtained — would be the available evidence of receptor expression at the membrane. That assay is not reported on this page.

A Registry part named “mCherry NLS” is mislabelled

BBa_25V0ZX3W is named “mCherry NLS”, but its sequence is identical to plain mCherry with no nuclear localisation signal. We identified this during the duplicate check and selected BBa_K4177005 instead.

In-silico sequence QC

Computational checks performed before synthesis. This is not experimental Sanger or whole-plasmid sequencing.

  • Reading frame Checked in silico
  • Internal stop codons Checked in silico — none in the intended ORFs
  • Kozak context Checked in silico
  • Domain order Checked in silico against the two-module architectures
  • Junction integrity Checked in silico
  • Cloning-site checks Checked in silico for the intended pcDNA3.1(+) strategy
  • Tandem repeats / homopolymers Screened in silico
  • Translation against FlyBase reference All eleven codon-optimised CDSs encode proteins identical to the reference
  • Junction-created restriction sites No new sites at any junction in any of the ten composites; each composite’s site count equals the sum of its sub-parts

In-silico sequence QC is computational verification before synthesis, not experimental sequence confirmation. Wet-lab sequencing belongs on Experiments and Results.
